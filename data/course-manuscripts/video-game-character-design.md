## 02 — Character Design in a Playable Context

A game character serves several purposes at once: it communicates personality, enables gameplay actions, remains recognizable during motion and helps players understand the world. A striking illustration can fail in a game if it becomes unreadable at normal camera distance or cannot be animated through required actions. Character design therefore combines narrative role, silhouette, anatomy, costume, topology and technical constraints.

Learners should interpret a game brief, write a character role and visual language, explore silhouettes, build reference sheets, plan clothing and equipment, consider diverse users, map movement requirements, create consistent turnaround views, explain basic animation handoff and test readability in a sample game scene. The final character is fictional and independently designed.

**Eight-hour study route:** Introduction 20 minutes; eight sections on pages 3–10 at 45 minutes each (360 minutes); original character case on page 11 for 60 minutes; final portfolio assessment on page 12 for 40 minutes. Total 480 minutes. Pencil sketches or digital illustration tools are acceptable; a full game engine is not required.

**Starter:** Sketch three distinct silhouettes for a courier in a fictional exploration game. Explain what each shape says about speed, equipment and personality.

## 03 — Brief, Gameplay Role and Player Reading

Start with what the character does, not what accessories look impressive. Does the character climb, deliver, solve puzzles, fight, heal or guide the player? Required actions shape anatomy, silhouette, gear and animation. A fast-moving character may need readable limbs and a visible carrying system. A large costume may express personality but hide important movement or collision cues.

Specify audience, genre, camera distance, target platform, setting, tone and performance budget. Third-person gameplay, first-person view and a small mobile sprite have different visual requirements. The character should be identifiable in the actual game context. Avoid designing details that disappear when displayed at 80 pixels tall.

Represent personality through coherent choices. A careful technician might have organized tools and a balanced stance; a curious courier might carry a notebook and a flexible bag. These are design suggestions, not stereotypes about real occupations or demographic groups. Record what the player should infer from the design and then test those assumptions.

**Fictional example:** A player controls a messenger delivering repair parts through a small settlement. The character must run, climb ladders and carry a package; a huge rigid shoulder ornament would obstruct those poses.

**Practice:** Write a seven-field character brief and three questions to test silhouette recognition.

## 04 — Silhouettes, Shape Language and Readability

Silhouette is the outer contour of a character. Strong silhouettes can help users distinguish roles quickly, particularly during fast movement or at small scale. Explore broad proportions and readable negative spaces before refining surface patterns. A design that relies on tiny accessories may collapse into an indistinct outline at actual play size.

Shape language is a creative shorthand, not a scientific personality test. Rounded forms may suggest softness, angular forms energy or tension, and stable rectangles dependability in some visual contexts. These associations are culturally and stylistically contingent. Choose shapes for the intended world and evaluate them with real readers instead of assuming every audience interprets them identically.

Use thumbnail exploration. Draw at least twelve silhouettes with meaningful changes in torso shape, bag location, head outline and stance. Select three for evaluation against gameplay, narrative and animation. Compare the figures as black shapes without color, then inspect them at target resolution.

**Fictional example:** A courier's large messenger bag is visible from the side but disappears in the front silhouette. A repositioned strap and bag improve recognition without adding unrelated decorations.

**Practice:** Create twelve 30-second silhouettes and choose three based on stated design criteria.

## 05 — Anatomy, Proportion and Pose Design

Anatomical knowledge supports believable movement even in highly stylized figures. Establish head-to-body proportions, shoulder and hip relationships, joint locations, hands, feet and balance. Intentional stylization can exaggerate features without producing accidental deformities. A character needs a consistent skeletal logic for turns, jumps and object contact.

Draw action poses rather than only a neutral front view. The courier should hold a parcel securely, move with weight over the support foot and bend without limbs passing through the bag. Assess silhouette clarity and whether the camera can reveal the action. Count fingers and check attachment points when drawing detailed hands; these small mistakes can distract from the gameplay message.

A pose sheet should show idle, walk, run, climb, carry and interaction states, with variations for game needs. Clothing must not make the character unable to perform the intended motion. For 3D rigs, joint placement and deformation should be considered during modeling rather than left to the animator as an unexplained problem.

**Fictional example:** The messenger bag intersects the elbow during a climbing pose. The design is adjusted before detailed final illustration.

**Practice:** Draw six movement silhouettes and note a plausible contact point in each.

## 06 — Costume, Props and Visual Storytelling

Costume and props convey role, setting and history. A delivery character might wear practical shoes, carry a bag and use a small map device. Design each detail to support character, action or world context. Do not add accessories that conflict with movement merely to fill empty space. Props should be dimensioned relative to the figure and remain recognizable across front, side and back views.

Colors can group costume elements and indicate useful information, but should not be the only way players distinguish critical characters. Consider contrast against the expected environments. A dark character may disappear in a dark cave without appropriate lighting or outline separation. Avoid misleading cultural appropriation and document sources of inspiration. Do not copy existing game character outfits or trademarked emblems.

Material choices affect movement. Loose fabrics and rigid accessories require different animation or simulation techniques. If a bag strap changes sides between frames, the design will appear inconsistent. A character sheet should state the approved costume, carry-side convention and fixed colors.

**Fictional example:** The courier has a bright scarf that aids identification but obscures a ladder-climbing animation. The scarf is shortened or repositioned after movement testing.

**Practice:** Design a three-color costume, two functional props and a continuity checklist.

## 07 — Character Sheets, Turnarounds and Expression

A character sheet communicates a design to other artists. It typically includes a front view, profile, back view, three-quarter pose, palette, materials, prop details and expressions. Important landmarks should line up across views. An inconsistent head shape between front and profile can create modeling or animation problems.

Turnarounds support three-dimensional thinking. They should preserve proportions, equipment placement and the shape of clothing. Expression sheets can show calm, surprised, worried and delighted without changing a character's identity. Avoid using facial expressions as the only gameplay cue; combine them with pose, motion and context where necessary.

Prepare a clear file package. Name illustrations and layers consistently, record scale or resolution, and distinguish approved design from exploratory options. An animator should not have to infer whether the bag is on the left or right shoulder or whether the hat is optional.

**Fictional example:** The courier's hat visor appears at different angles between orthographic views. The artist revises the turnaround and checks it again.

**Practice:** Create a four-view turnaround, four expressions and a prop reference sheet.

## 08 — Animation, Rigging and Game-Engine Constraints

A game character is often a real-time asset. Animation needs clean joint behavior and a rig or sprite system suited to actions. For 3D, topology around shoulders, elbows, knees and the mouth can influence deformation; for 2D, layered cutout rigs or sprite sheets need consistent pivots and frame boundaries. The design stage should anticipate these requirements.

Performance constraints matter. Excessive geometric detail, material variety and texture sizes may reduce frame rate on target hardware. The correct budget depends on platform, camera distance and scene complexity, so do not impose one universal polygon limit. Level of detail, texture packing and rig complexity may be used where supported, but each requires testing.

Test the character in context. A hero model on a plain studio background may be readable, yet the same character can disappear among complex foliage. Check scale, lighting, collision geometry, animation transitions and silhouette during gameplay. A visual model is not proof that its controls or animations operate correctly.

**Fictional example:** The courier has a swinging bag that clips through a doorframe. The team tests changes to animation, dimensions or collision behavior.

**Practice:** Write a technical handoff with action list, moving accessories, performance assumptions and five test cases.

## 09 — Diversity, Accessibility and Responsible Design

Character design affects who feels represented and how players interpret others. Avoid reducing complex identities to stereotypes or using disabilities as decorative threats. Research cultural and contextual references accurately and seek informed review when appropriate. Representation should respect people while allowing diverse fictional personalities.

Accessibility includes visual recognition and gameplay communication. Character status should be conveyed through more than color; silhouettes, animations, labels or audio can help. Fast-moving effects should not obscure critical enemy or ally roles. Consider players with limited vision, hearing or motor abilities in the overall game system.

Ethical design avoids unauthorized use of real faces, trademarks or protected characters. Generated concept art should be reviewed for originality and provenance. A studio should clearly distinguish fictional test figures from actual people and avoid claims of endorsement by other creative owners.

**Fictional example:** A game uses two team colors that become indistinguishable in grayscale. The artist adds different costumes and clear symbols to preserve recognition.

**Practice:** Audit a character concept for stereotypes, readability, cultural references and ownership risks.

## 10 — Iteration, Portfolio and Quality Review

Character work should be evaluated against the brief rather than personal taste alone. Test recognition at actual target size, role comprehension, pose clarity and consistency across views. Keep earlier iterations so the team can see why changes occurred. A highly detailed concept may need simplification to improve performance or animation reliability.

Quality assurance includes anatomy, joints, finger counts, hands gripping props, feet meeting the floor, clothing continuity, accessory placement and palette accuracy. Inspect multiple views and movement poses, not only an attractive hero image. For production, separate artistic approval from technical asset testing.

A portfolio should show the process: brief, silhouettes, selected concepts, turnaround, color and expression studies, action poses and an implementation note. Do not present a final illustration as proof of a production-ready rig. Name the untested dependencies and whether the game engine or technical artist has reviewed the asset.

**Fictional example:** The courier's bag changes sides in two views. The correction is recorded in the design history before art is submitted.

**Practice:** Create a fifteen-point QA checklist and evaluate a draft against five original criteria.

## 11 — Worked Project: The Community Courier

**Entirely fictional original character.** Design a friendly courier for a third-person exploration and delivery game. The character carries repair parts, climbs short ladders and crosses outdoor settlements. The target is a readable design on both medium-size desktop screens and small mobile gameplay previews. The project does not borrow recognizable characters from other games.

Develop a brief, twelve silhouettes and three selected directions. Choose one and draw a front, side, back and three-quarter turnaround. Add six action poses: idle, walking, running, carrying a parcel, climbing and delivering. Create a backpack or messenger bag reference and keep the strap orientation consistent. Check contact between hands and parcel, foot balance and collision risks.

**Illustrative screen-size test:** At 100% asset height, a scarf detail may be readable; at 25% displayed height, it may disappear. This does not mean the graphic contains only 25% of its pixels in every rendering system: the practical task is to inspect the actual target size.

Create a palette with no more than four functional colors and a grayscale recognition test. Outline a simple 3D or 2D implementation path and identify which features require technical validation.

**Deliverables:** Character brief, silhouette sheet, turnaround, action poses, prop details, palette, technical handoff and a short revision log.

## 12 — Knowledge Check and Character Portfolio

**Self-test:** (1) Why design a game character for gameplay rather than illustration alone? (2) What makes a silhouette readable? (3) Why can shape language be culturally variable? (4) What anatomical landmarks matter during action poses? (5) Why must costume and props support movement? (6) How do model sheets help technical teams? (7) What does a rig need to support? (8) Why are polygon budgets context-dependent? (9) How can character status be communicated without color alone? (10) What should a production portfolio disclose about untested assets?

**Portfolio project:** Create an original playable-character concept with a seven-field brief, twelve silhouettes, a four-view turnaround, expressions and six movement poses. Add a costume specification, prop reference, grayscale test, technical budget assumptions, ethical review and revision notes. Mark any unsolved engine or rigging work clearly.

**Practice rubric:** Role and research 15%; silhouette and shape 20%; anatomy and action 20%; costume and continuity 15%; technical readiness 15%; accessibility, originality and review 15%. This is study feedback, not an external industry qualification or activated MAIC exam.

**Further resources:** Game Accessibility Guidelines (https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/); IGDA educational resources (https://igda.org/resources-archive/).
