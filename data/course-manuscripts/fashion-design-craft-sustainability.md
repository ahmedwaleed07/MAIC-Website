## 02 — Fashion Design Beyond Appearance

Fashion design develops clothing and related products by considering form, material, construction, comfort, culture, user requirements and distribution. The craft process transforms a concept into a usable product through research, patternmaking, sampling and fit testing. Sustainability asks how choices influence resource use, labor, product life and recovery. An appealing style does not automatically satisfy those responsibilities.

Learners should be able to write a design brief, examine dress and cultural context respectfully, select textiles for performance, create a specification, understand basic patterns, plan a prototype, assess fit and manufacturing quality, document labor and environmental questions, explore repair and reuse, and propose a transparent capsule collection. The course requires original work and careful sourcing, not tracing another fashion house's designs.

**Eight-hour guided schedule:** Introduction 20 minutes; eight sections on pages 3–10 at 45 minutes each (360 minutes); garment case on page 11 for 60 minutes; review on page 12 for 40 minutes. Total 480 minutes including sketches and written exercises. Learners may use paper, fabric remnants or digital drawings without specialized equipment.

**Starter:** Sketch a simple shirt for frequent daily wear. Note who will use it, climate, movement, cleaning, repair and likely service life before selecting a fashion color.

## 03 — People, Culture and the Fashion Brief

A design brief specifies the wearer, use case, fit expectations, season, price range, production constraints and desired identity. Research may involve observation, interviews, fitting feedback and material studies. Treat body-size variation respectfully; a single fit model cannot represent every wearer. Avoid claiming universal suitability from one test or making assumptions about people's preferences based only on gender, age or nationality.

Clothing carries cultural meaning. Historical silhouettes, traditional textiles and symbols can provide context, but designers should identify sources and consider community perspectives rather than copying culturally meaningful elements without acknowledgement. A mood board helps convey direction but cannot replace fit requirements or technical specifications. Keep a distinction between inspiration and authorized use of protected prints or marks.

Write a short value proposition: who the garment serves and which functional benefit it delivers. A work shirt may prioritize comfortable movement and durable seams; ceremonial clothing may emphasize precise silhouette and finish. If the brief claims water resistance or exceptional durability, plan a relevant performance test.

**Fictional case:** A commuter needs a lightweight overshirt that can be repaired and washed frequently. The designer records climate, size range, pocket function and fabric-care requirements.

**Practice:** Draft a wearer profile as a research hypothesis, a ten-field garment brief and two needs that remain unverified.

## 04 — Textiles, Fibers and Material Performance

Fibers and fabrics differ in strength, breathability, stretch, recovery, drape, moisture behavior and maintenance. Natural, regenerated and synthetic materials each have tradeoffs. Cotton's familiar feel does not prove low environmental impact; polyester's durability does not guarantee that it is the right material for every garment. The effects depend on sourcing, processing, dyeing, care and actual use.

Textile structure influences behavior. Woven fabrics have intersecting yarns and generally different stretch properties from knitted structures. Finishing, coatings and blends can alter hand feel and performance, but may complicate recycling. Inspect supplier specifications for composition, width, shrinkage and care, and use test swatches to verify assumptions. A digital texture is not evidence of actual fabric behavior.

Choose fabric against the wearer and the pattern. A stiff woven material may not follow a silhouette designed for elastic knit. Seam type and allowances depend on fabric and construction. Where chemical or product safety requirements apply, obtain appropriate manufacturer evidence and jurisdiction-specific compliance review.

**Fictional example:** Two fabrics are proposed for an overshirt. Fabric A has low stretch and strong structure; Fabric B has more elasticity but requires a different sewing approach. Neither is declared superior without use-case and performance tests.

**Practice:** Create a comparison sheet with fiber content, construction, expected drape, care and evidence gaps.

## 05 — Sketches, Patterns, Fit and Prototypes

A fashion illustration expresses silhouette and intent. A technical flat drawing communicates construction features such as collar, pockets, openings, seams and hems. Neither illustration establishes fit. Pattern pieces translate the intended shape into cut fabric components. Grain direction, seam allowances and consistent measurement points influence assembly. A prototype or toile allows the designer to test proportion and movement before committing to final fabric.

Fit evaluation should include static and dynamic tasks. A garment may look appropriate while standing yet restrict shoulder movement or sitting. Record feedback from relevant wearers and identify whether the issue lies in size, pattern, fabric or construction. Change one meaningful design feature at a time where possible and preserve prototype versions.

Production instructions need clarity. A tech pack can specify flat sketches, materials, measurements, tolerance targets, seam types, trims, care labels and approved colors. A manufacturer must know which details are fixed and which require confirmation. The goal is repeatability, not simply attractive presentation.

**Fictional example:** An overshirt sleeve restricts reaching forward. The team adjusts the pattern and tests the revised sample; a larger-looking illustration is not proof of improved mobility.

**Practice:** Sketch front and back flats, list five pattern pieces, and write a three-step movement-fit test.

## 06 — Construction, Quality and Responsible Craft

Craft quality depends on accurate cutting, suitable seams, stable fasteners, clean finishing and repeatable workmanship. Different textiles and uses require different construction techniques. Stitch strength, seam slippage and wash behavior may need testing; a decorative seam can fail if it is placed under repeated tension. Plan an inspection checklist that includes measurements, seam appearance, label information, dangerous sharp components and production defects.

Responsible manufacturing includes people as well as materials. Supply chains can involve subcontracting, overtime, wages, worker safety and access to complaints mechanisms. A brand's statement of ethics is not proof of actual conditions. Supplier documentation, credible audits and worker-centered due diligence are different sources of evidence and have limitations. Avoid inventing a certification that a workshop has not earned.

Designers can improve communication by defining realistic tolerances and recording revisions. A changed pocket position should be documented in the tech pack before production. Quality control may identify a problem, but root-cause correction and retesting are necessary to avoid repeated defects.

**Fictional example:** A batch of shirts has buttons detaching after washing. The team reviews attachment methods and conducts appropriate tests rather than dismissing the issue as cosmetic.

**Practice:** Create a ten-point product QA checklist and a supplier-question list covering labor and process transparency.

## 07 — Environmental Impacts Across Garment Life

Garment impacts arise from fiber production, yarn and fabric manufacture, dyeing, transport, use and disposal. The relative importance of stages depends on the actual textile and user behavior. A light garment is not automatically low impact if it fails rapidly; a longer-lasting garment may reduce replacement demand. A life-cycle comparison needs a common functional unit, such as providing a defined number of suitable wears under comparable care conditions.

Water, energy, chemical management, microfibers, waste and climate effects may move in different directions. Avoid reducing sustainability to one colored score without a documented method. Care instructions can affect service life and use-stage demand; actual washing and drying practices vary. Some environmental data are estimates and should be labeled as such.

Fashion businesses should address excess production and discarded inventory. Designing for repair, timeless functionality, durable construction and accurate demand forecasting can support waste prevention. Yet a repair program needs usable parts and practical economics. A recycled-content label does not prove the finished garment can be recycled.

**Fictional example:** A shirt lasts 100 wears instead of 50. Its hypothetical production impact per wear may decrease if all other assumptions are equal, but actual impacts require more data.

**Practice:** Draw a garment life-cycle map and list five impact indicators with explicit evidence needs.

## 08 — Circular Fashion: Repair, Reuse and Material Recovery

Circular strategies seek to keep clothing useful through care, repair, resale, rental, remanufacturing and appropriate textile recovery. A durable design with accessible seams and replaceable buttons may make repair easier. A resale platform can extend use when demand exists, but additional transport and handling should be included in realistic comparisons.

Design for disassembly can improve separation of trims and materials, yet real recycling infrastructure for complex textile blends remains limited or variable. A “recyclable” claim should specify pathway and market evidence. Return schemes require collection, sorting and transparent outcomes. Do not describe donation as guaranteed reuse; some items may remain unsold or require disposal.

Consider product information that makes maintenance and repair practical. Care labels, spare parts and understandable repair instructions can help users. Avoid planned obsolescence and unsupported assertions about perfect circularity. When comparing schemes, track actual garment use, returns, repair rates and material recovery rather than only campaign participation.

**Fictional example:** An overshirt includes replaceable buttons and a patch kit. A repair demonstration documents whether typical damage can be fixed safely and affordably.

**Practice:** Design a garment return journey and identify three realistic losses or failure points.

## 09 — Branding, Honest Claims and Collection Planning

A fashion collection needs consistent visual identity, audience relevance, product differentiation and financially realistic production. A capsule collection may reduce complexity by reusing patterns and material families, but it does not automatically decrease resource use if total output or waste increases. Test the design's useful features rather than assuming a small product range proves sustainability.

Environmental and labor claims must be specific and verifiable. “Made with 40% recycled fiber” requires traceable evidence defining which component and mass basis the statement covers. “Ethically made” can obscure important conditions if no process is disclosed. Brand communications should distinguish documented supplier information from expectations, goals and independent certification.

Create a clear product information sheet with composition, care, sizing, origin details as legally required, repair options and evidence-backed statements. Examine product photography for accuracy: a digitally changed color may misrepresent what arrives. Respect original designs, artwork licenses and third-party brand marks.

**Fictional example:** An ad calls a cotton blend “zero-impact fashion.” The designer withdraws that claim and asks for substantiated specific information instead.

**Practice:** Draft two responsible product labels and a claims-verification register.

## 10 — Sampling, Cost, Delivery and Evaluation

A concept becomes a product through samples, testing, supplier planning, costing and quality acceptance. Cost includes materials, trims, cutting, assembly, packaging, transport, returns and overhead. A low fabric price alone does not establish an affordable retail product. Map dependencies such as approved fit before grading sizes or buying full production quantities.

A basic bill of materials identifies each component and estimated quantity. Supplier lead times and minimum orders affect inventory risk. Record the exact sample version and revision history. A pilot batch can expose sewing defects, fit variation or inaccurate descriptions, but conclusions require an appropriate sample and testing method.

Select meaningful product metrics: defect rate, return reasons, verified fit satisfaction, repairability and actual inventory waste. Marketing likes are not a substitute for product quality. If a critical safety or quality problem appears, stop distribution and address it before expansion. Distinguish fictional classroom cost exercises from real supplier quotes.

**Illustrative arithmetic:** A training overshirt uses 2 meters of fabric at $6/m, $3 trims and $12 labor. The direct component/labor subtotal is $12 + $3 + $12 = $27, excluding overhead, shipping and taxes.

**Practice:** Build a ten-line bill of materials and a sample approval log.

## 11 — Worked Case: A Repairable Overshirt

**Entirely fictional studio project.** A small clothing brand proposes a relaxed everyday overshirt for commuters. The design uses a simple collar, two pockets and replaceable buttons. Two fabric candidates differ in stretch, durability assumptions and wash care. The goal is wearable function, practical repair and credible product information, not a guaranteed environmental claim.

Start with a wearer brief and two front/back sketches. Develop a technical flat and list pattern components. Define a fit test involving reaching forward, lifting the arms and sitting. Prepare an initial sample plan with a measurement sheet and revision register. Compare textiles across structure, care and performance evidence. Specify an accessible seam or fastener strategy that permits button replacement.

**Illustrative figures:** If fabric costs $6/m and 2 meters are required, fabric costs $12. With $3 in trims and $12 labor, the direct subtotal is $27. For 20 proposed samples, that subtotal would be $540 if every sample used identical quantities and no waste, overhead or extra work. These numbers are not a supplier quote or final garment retail price.

Design a six-field care/repair information card and a fair marketing description. Review sourcing questions without claiming unverified labor certifications. Identify how returns and wear testing could change the design decision.

**Deliverables:** Brief, technical flats, sample measurements, two material options, repair plan, cost sheet, QA checklist and transparent claims review.

## 12 — Knowledge Check and Original Fashion Portfolio

**Self-test:** (1) How do garment styling and functional design differ? (2) What belongs in a wearer brief? (3) How do woven and knitted structures differ? (4) Why is fit tested in movement? (5) What information belongs in a technical pack? (6) Why do working conditions matter to responsible fashion? (7) What is a reasonable functional unit for garment impact comparisons? (8) Why is recycled content not the same as garment recyclability? (9) What must a sustainability claim identify? (10) Why must a sample be approved before large production?

**Final practice:** Develop an original capsule concept with one repairable overshirt as the anchor item. Supply a brief, design sketches, material comparison, technical detail sheet, sampling and fit plan, quality criteria, life-cycle questions, responsible claims check and illustrative direct-cost estimate. State the source and limits of every numeric claim.

**Practice rubric:** User and cultural context 15%; material and construction logic 20%; technical and fit testing 20%; environmental and labor evidence 20%; repair and circularity 10%; cost, communication and originality 15%. Practice completion is not an official professional certification or MAIC exam result.
