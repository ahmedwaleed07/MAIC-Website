## 02 — Course Route, Goals and Project Files

Editing turns recorded material into a comprehensible sequence. The editor selects, orders and times shots, maintains meaning, controls sound and prepares a reliable deliverable. iMovie and DaVinci Resolve differ in depth and interface, yet both depend on clear source organization, purposeful cuts and accurate final checks. This course teaches transferable decisions alongside practice in the two applications; it is not a replacement for the vendors' full technical documentation.

By the end, learners should be able to organize original media, identify source and timeline frame rates, assemble and trim a rough cut, manage titles and sound, apply restrained color corrections, export an appropriate file and verify the final output. They should also recognize the limits of cross-application interchange: a movie export is not an editable project, and a native project from one application should not be assumed to open in the other.

**Eight-hour study path:** orientation (20 minutes); eight technique sections on pages 3–10 (35 minutes each, or 280 minutes); worked edit on page 11 (90 minutes); and assessment plus revision on page 12 (90 minutes). Total: 480 minutes. Use your own rights-cleared test clips, narration and music, or record a fictional practice scene. Never present invented examples as a real documentary event.

**Getting started:** Make a folder containing original camera files, permitted audio, graphics, references, project files and exports. Preserve unaltered originals; work on copies where appropriate. Note source resolution, frame rate and duration. A successful learning edit is understandable, accurate and reproducible, not merely attractive.

## 03 — Plan the Edit and Organize Source Media

Begin with a brief stating audience, intended message, expected running time and where the video will play. Inspect every source before placing it on the timeline. Label clips by meaningful content, not just camera-generated filenames. Group media into interview, demonstration, establishing shots, supplementary images, audio, graphics and permissions. Note which recorded statements are factual and which are illustrative. In a documentary project, never make an edit that falsely changes the order or cause of real events.

A rough paper edit or outline might use a 10-second introduction, 40-second explanation, 20-second demonstration and 10-second ending, subject to actual footage and the target duration. The story should determine timing rather than a template imposed regardless of content. Identify what viewers must hear and which images add information. Avoid filling gaps with unrelated stock footage that implies an event was filmed when it was not.

Keep originals and a project backup separate from temporary caches and exported movies. A native project usually references or stores media in application-specific ways; moving or renaming files can create missing-media issues. Document file locations before starting. If a file is missing, locate the original or a verified replacement; do not silently substitute a different event. Inspect ownership and licensed use of music, photographs and fonts before including them.

**Example:** A 75-second community-center notice needs its verified schedule, entrance shot, presenter, accessible titles and official contact. A scenic shot is optional; the correct closing time is not.

**Practice:** Build a six-clip logging table with source path, content, technical format, rights and use in the edit. Write a timeline outline and identify one claim that needs fact checking.

## 04 — iMovie on Mac: Import, Project and Timeline

iMovie is an accessible editor for assembling and polishing everyday films. The Mac version provides a media browser, preview viewer and timeline. Device and software versions differ: this section describes the Mac workflow, and learners should consult Apple's current help for controls on iPhone or iPad. Create a movie project, import the test footage, inspect the media in the browser and place selected material in the timeline. Save a working title that distinguishes the editable project from the final exported file.

Choose the best useful portion of each shot rather than importing every second into the finished sequence. Move timeline clips to establish an intelligible order. For a basic first pass, place an opening context shot, the essential demonstration, a short supporting detail and a closing instruction. Play the sequence through once without effects. If a viewer cannot understand the narrative now, transitions will not solve it.

On Mac, select a clip to adjust it. Trimming an edge shortens or extends the visible portion within available source handles; moving the clip changes its place in the sequence. Splitting creates two independently editable sections at the playhead. Apple documents **Modify > Split Clip** and **Command-B** as one Mac workflow. Interface placement may vary by version. Remember that editing usually changes the project arrangement; it does not modify the original recorded file.

**Troubleshooting:** If a clip cannot extend further, the source may have no remaining frames at that boundary. If a sudden cut seems confusing, try a clearer establishing shot before adding a transition. Check the selected audio when moving or shortening video.

**Practice:** Import at least four clips, arrange them into a 30-second sequence, trim one clip, split another and confirm the original files remain intact.

## 05 — iMovie: Trim, Split, Transitions and Pacing

A cut changes from one image to another without an optical transition. It often communicates more clearly than a decorative effect. Use trimming to remove delays before an action or after it finishes. Use a split when a continuous recording contains a passage to remove or when separate sections need different treatment. Review both sides of every cut for continuity, such as a presenter holding the same object, a consistent direction of movement and reasonable lighting.

In iMovie on Mac, Apple's help describes dragging a transition between adjacent clips from the Transitions browser; a cross-dissolve can also be added using the appropriate edit control. A dissolve blends one shot into another but consumes overlapping available material. Avoid assuming any transition can have unlimited duration: sufficient adjacent source material is needed. Do not add a dissolve to disguise a factual discontinuity or to make an old scene appear contemporaneous with a new one.

Pacing is an editorial judgment. A tutorial may require longer shots to show hand movements, while an introductory montage can use short clips without obscuring meaning. Play the work at normal speed on the intended kind of screen. Check that viewers have time to read graphics and that important actions do not begin before the shot appears.

**Worked example:** A 12-second shot contains two seconds of camera settling, six seconds of useful demonstration and four seconds after the action. A first trim might retain seconds 2–8, yielding six useful seconds; check the visual start and end before accepting the cut.

**Practice:** Assemble two versions of the same four-shot demonstration, one with cuts and one with a single justified dissolve. Explain which better preserves the chronology and why.

## 06 — iMovie: Sound, Titles, Color and Export

Sound often carries the video's most important information. Listen to dialogue for background noise, uneven loudness and clipped words. Set music below intelligible speech rather than increasing it to add excitement. Use source audio, narration and licensed music intentionally. If a clip's audio includes a name or number that matters, verify the recording rather than relying blindly on automatic transcription. Do not publish accidentally captured private conversations without addressing privacy and consent.

Titles should communicate information, not compete with it. Use short lines, legible contrast, and consistent placement. Place names, dates and times only when checked against original records. A title card that displays the wrong event date is a factual error even when spoken narration is accurate. Apply brightness and color adjustments carefully across related shots; avoid modifications that change documentary evidence into an untrue impression.

Before exporting, identify delivery orientation and size appropriate to the intended screen, and use iMovie's sharing/export interface to create a playable file. The output movie is not a backup of the editable project. Watch and listen to the resulting file outside the editor. Check the first and final frames, transitions, captions, spelling, audio balance and aspect ratio. If a platform recompresses the upload, inspect its displayed result as well.

**Example:** The voiceover says the opening hours are Saturday 9 a.m.–5 p.m., but the title says 9 a.m.–6 p.m. Correct the inconsistency against the official notice before exporting.

**Practice:** Add a title, balance narration and background audio, make one restrained visual adjustment and export a test. Record one issue discovered only during playback.

## 07 — DaVinci Resolve: Media Management and Project Setup

DaVinci Resolve offers specialized workspaces for media organization, editing, color, sound and delivery. The exact controls depend on version, edition and configuration. Blackmagic Design provides official training and manuals; this course describes a stable beginner workflow, not a guarantee that every feature exists in every edition. Begin by creating a distinct project, importing your rights-cleared clips, organizing them into bins and reviewing source formats. Verify that the project and timeline frame-rate choices suit the intended delivery before building a complex edit.

Use clearly named bins for interview, B-roll, graphics and audio. If the program cannot locate footage after moving the project, look for the original file locations and relink rather than substituting wrong clips. Proxy or optimized-media workflows can make editing smoother on less powerful machines, but temporary media should not replace the verified original source. Know where media lives and maintain separate backups.

A project database, project archive and rendered video are different things. A database holds editable project information, while a render is the finished sequence. Do not expect a video file alone to preserve timeline edits, color adjustments or audio automation. Record settings and keep an archive or export appropriate to the team’s handover requirements.

**Example:** The camera provides 25 fps footage. Choosing a 24 fps project by accident can introduce unwanted frame mapping or timing differences. Decide the intended delivery and technical workflow before editing; if uncertain, test a short sequence and inspect motion.

**Practice:** Create an empty project, import a small test folder, name three bins, inspect at least one clip's frame rate and explain how you would recover an offline media link.

## 08 — DaVinci Resolve: Cut and Edit Timeline Decisions

DaVinci Resolve's Cut and Edit workspaces can assemble a sequence, but an editor still must decide what belongs in it. Review source clips, mark usable in and out points where appropriate and create a rough cut on a timeline. Arrange the main talking points before adding supporting images. Use trim operations to refine where one clip ends and the next begins. Watch audio continuity: a visually smooth edit may create an abrupt interruption in speech or ambient sound.

Use separate tracks deliberately when layering titles, B-roll and audio. A second video layer can cover part of an interview while its audio continues, but the overlaid image must accurately illustrate the subject. If the secondary image is archival or staged, label it as needed. Split edits, such as hearing the next scene before the picture changes, may aid flow when they preserve truthful relationships. A transition should not hide an actual change in event or location.

Define version names, such as roughcut_01 and approvedcut_02, so feedback can reference a precise sequence. Avoid changing the only good version during experiments. If footage was recorded in different resolutions or aspect ratios, choose a deliberate crop or letterbox strategy and check that important content is not lost.

**Practical calculation:** A 90-second target assembled from six 20-second source clips requires selecting and trimming a total of 30 seconds across the combined 120-second sources, assuming no overlapping or added media. Real timing must then be checked by playback.

**Practice:** Make a 45-second rough cut with at least five shots. Explain two cuts where action continuity matters more than applying effects.

## 09 — DaVinci Resolve: Fairlight and Color Foundations

Edit audio before final delivery. The Fairlight workspace supports sound editing and mixing workflows. For a simple factual video, start by verifying the speaker's recorded words, eliminating unnecessary gaps and balancing dialogue against ambient or licensed music. Use audio meters and listen through an appropriate playback device. Excessive processing can create unnatural artifacts; if sound is unusable, the accurate remedy may be rerecording rather than aggressive correction.

Color work should establish consistent, readable footage rather than impose a fashionable grade regardless of content. Check exposure and white balance first. A primary correction can bring a darker shot closer to adjacent shots; a creative look is optional and should not make an educational demonstration ambiguous. The Color workspace provides deeper tools than iMovie's simplified adjustments, but beginners should make small controlled changes and assess the whole sequence, not only a single beautiful frame.

Use before-and-after comparison to detect clipping, unwanted color casts or differences in skin-tone representation. Do not remove visual evidence or manipulate factual scenes to make events appear unlike the original recording. Keep a version of the original for reference. Audio and color are both iterative processes: revisit them after picture timing is stable.

**Example:** Two interview angles recorded under different lamps have visibly different whites. The goal is credible consistency, not maximizing saturation. Check both clips against a neutral reference and the actual conditions.

**Practice:** Balance one dialogue passage and compare two neighboring images. Write down adjustments, artifacts noticed and reasons for retaining or reverting them.

## 10 — Resolve Delivery, Cross-Application Limits and QA

The Deliver workspace in DaVinci Resolve is used for rendering output appropriate to its destination. Choose the requested frame size, frame rate, format and codec based on the original material, target platform and available presets. One universally "best" output does not exist. Estimate whether the file size, quality and playback compatibility meet the recipient's needs. Inspect all delivery settings before rendering and verify the actual file afterward.

For both iMovie and Resolve, conduct a final three-part check. Content: are names, timing, location, on-screen numbers and footage provenance correct? Sound and image: can viewers hear speech, read captions and follow movement; does any crop hide relevant information? Technical: does the file open, play from first frame to last, and preserve intended aspect ratio? When making a correction, create a traceable new version rather than silently replacing a materially inaccurate work.

Cross-application caution: an exported iMovie movie can be imported into Resolve as a video file, but this normally flattens the edit and does not reproduce the original iMovie timeline structure. Similarly, a rendered Resolve movie will not automatically become a layered iMovie project. For genuine interchange, retain organized original media, edit notes and any project interchange features supported by your exact software versions. Never promise full editable round-trip compatibility without testing.

**Example:** A reviewer wants the subtitle font changed after a movie was flattened into a single video. The original editable project makes this practical; a flat video alone may require reconstructing the graphic or rebuilding part of the edit.

**Practice:** Write an export checklist and describe which files would be included in a responsible editor-to-editor handoff.

## 11 — Worked Case: Two Editors, One Verified Service Video

**Entirely fictional case.** A community learning center issues a dated notice that it will test new Saturday opening hours for six weeks. You have eight video clips: one exterior view, one presenter explanation, two service demonstrations, three supporting details and one closing view. You also have a voice recording, a verified schedule graphic and music licensed for the task. The assignment is a coherent 60–90-second public-service video. The notice does not establish permanent opening or a guaranteed rise in attendance.

First write a shot and claim table. Confirm the date, new hours and services from the source notice. In **iMovie on Mac**, import the clips, arrange them, trim dead space, use restrained transitions, balance sound, place the verified graphic and export a draft movie. Review the exported file rather than judging only the timeline. Note any inaccurate caption, overly fast graphic or clipped word.

For a parallel exercise in **DaVinci Resolve**, import the same original media into bins, choose a suitable timeline, assemble the same sequence on the Edit or Cut page, then review speech in Fairlight and adjust visual consistency on the Color page. Render a review file from Deliver. Compare storytelling, legibility and audio rather than assuming the more complex interface automatically produces a better film.

**Illustrative arithmetic:** If the final cut contains clips lasting 9, 11, 8, 12, 10 and 15 seconds, the cut total is 65 seconds before any overlapping transition or added title duration. A one-second overlap at a dissolve can alter final running time depending on the editing method; verify the actual timeline readout.

**Exercise:** Prepare both edit plans, annotate each source clip's role, identify three rights/consent checks and draft a correction note for a wrong closing hour discovered after export.

## 12 — Knowledge Check, Editing Assignment and References

**Self-test:** (1) Why preserve original source media? (2) How is an editable project different from an exported movie? (3) What does trimming change? (4) Why can a long dissolve be impossible without source handles? (5) What does an iMovie split enable? (6) What is a bin in Resolve? (7) What kind of issue can Fairlight help correct? (8) Why match exposure before applying a creative look? (9) Why does a rendered iMovie video not recreate the original editable timeline in Resolve? (10) What should be verified after exporting?

**Final practical assignment:** Edit or plan a 60–90-second fictional community-center service announcement from rights-cleared footage. Submit a brief, source/rights log, shot list, rough-cut timeline, screenshot or description of one trim, one sound adjustment, one checked title graphic, and the final deliverable or an annotated timeline if software is unavailable. Where possible, complete the same story in both iMovie and Resolve and compare the results in 150–200 words. Record at least one problem corrected during QA.

**Self-assessment rubric:** Purpose and factual accuracy 25%; shot selection and pacing 20%; technical source organization 15%; audio and captions 15%; visual consistency 10%; export verification and revision record 15%.
