## 02 — Course Route and Learning Outcomes

Journalism is the disciplined process of finding facts, assessing their public importance, verifying evidence and communicating an accurate account. This course develops a reporting workflow rather than a collection of attractive writing techniques. A fluent assertion is not necessarily an accurate or verified assertion; the reporter must identify what supports it, what remains uncertain and whether the public benefit of publication outweighs foreseeable harm.

By completing the readings and exercises, the learner should be able to: identify a testable public-interest question; distinguish observation, allegation and interpretation; plan independent verification; conduct a neutral interview; assess documents and numerical claims; write a concise attributed story; and document an editorial correction. All examples are fictional unless an external reference is explicitly identified. Learners should never represent invented scenes, witnesses or quotations as real reporting.

**Study route:** introductory orientation 30 minutes; eight learning sections (pages 3–10) one hour each; worked reporting simulation (page 11) two hours; final exercise and self-assessment (page 12) one and a half hours. The total is 12 hours including the required practice, not merely the time taken to read these pages.

**Practice:** Identify a public-service question that could be checked using at least two independent sources. Write the evidence required before publishing a headline.

## 03 — Public Interest, News Judgment and Story Selection

A story deserves investigation when it can help people understand an important development, make an informed decision, hold an institution accountable or respond to a real risk. Attention and outrage are not substitutes for public interest. The reporting question should be specific enough to verify. Instead of asking whether a city has failed everyone, investigate what service was promised, what was delivered, where the difference occurred, who was affected and what the responsible authority can document.

Separate the event from claims about that event. A witness may truthfully report having heard an allegation without knowing whether it is accurate. A spokesperson may accurately describe an institution's position without resolving an underlying dispute. The reporter must not let an emotionally strong statement outrun the evidence. Evaluate likely harm before naming private individuals, sharing addresses or repeating unverified accusations.

A useful initial story brief states the proposed central question, the public affected, the available records, two independent sources, the unresolved facts and a realistic publication condition. Editorial independence means sources and commercial sponsors cannot purchase factual conclusions or silently control story selection.

**Fictional example:** A company announces 200 jobs. Determine whether those jobs are projected, authorized, advertised or already filled. The headline should reflect the verified category.

**Practice:** Draft a neutral story question and list three facts that could change your initial angle.

## 04 — Sources, Corroboration and Attribution

Assess a source for firsthand knowledge, access to records, relevant competence, incentives and possible conflicts. Authority in one subject does not automatically make a person authoritative in another. Differentiate original records, direct observation, firsthand accounts, institutional statements and secondary summaries. Two social accounts repeating a single unknown post do not provide two independent confirmations.

Record precisely what each source establishes. A dated official notice can support its stated contents, but it may not prove that a promised action was completed. An affected resident can establish an experienced delay, not necessarily the regional average. Seek independent documentary corroboration where feasible, particularly before making a serious allegation. A useful evidence log includes the claim, source, date, attribution conditions, file location, confirmation status and outstanding questions.

Use accurate quotation and paraphrase. Do not fabricate words, shift meaning by removing crucial context, or label a claim as proven when it remains disputed. Agree clearly on background or confidential-source conditions before an interview. Apply suitable security measures to protect sensitive source information, and follow applicable newsroom rules.

**Fictional example:** A witness reports a blocked road; a transport authority posts a closure advisory; images show barriers. Verify location and timestamps before claiming all sources describe the same event.

**Practice:** Make a source matrix for five claims. Explain which are independently confirmed and which still require investigation.

## 05 — Reporting Plans, Observation and Fair Response

Effective field reporting converts a broad claim into precise evidence tasks. Identify the affected people, relevant records, officials with direct responsibility and independent specialists who can test the explanation. Look for evidence that could disprove your working hypothesis. Before reporting serious allegations involving an identifiable person or organization, offer a fair opportunity to address the material claims when feasible. Report the response accurately and explain meaningful limits without pretending unsupported denials and well-established facts have equal evidentiary weight.

Distinguish what you observed from what others told you. Record dates, times, locations, names, spellings, direct quotations and the circumstances of each observation. Never imply firsthand presence where you only relied on a document or a third-party account. Keep recordings in their original form where appropriate. Explain how photographs, captions and clips were obtained and check the context of visible scenes.

Consider privacy, safety and consent. Avoid unnecessary identification of minors, vulnerable people or confidential locations. For recording and publication, comply with applicable law and ethics standards; jurisdiction and circumstances can change the rule. Do not pressure distressed people to repeat traumatic details simply for a more dramatic quotation.

**Fictional example:** Residents report bus delays. Compare schedule documents, dated observations, operator explanations and the effect of any recent route change.

**Practice:** Build a six-step reporting plan that includes an opposing or alternative explanation.

## 06 — Documents, Data and Digital Evidence

A document can be compelling evidence yet still be incomplete, outdated or incorrectly described. Check its issuing body, publication date, version, effective period and whether it has been amended. Distinguish a proposed policy from an adopted decision and a projection from a measured result. Retain traceable original material and document the basis for subsequent transformations. A screenshot may omit context; compare it to an original release or an independently corroborated archive when possible.

Before using numbers, define the unit, population, denominator and time period. Verify that categories have not been combined inconsistently and check whether missing observations change the conclusion. Rates are not comparable merely because their labels are similar. Correlation does not by itself establish causation. When translating figures or converting units, show enough arithmetic for another reviewer to reproduce the result. A neat chart cannot cure an incorrect denominator.

Images and video need provenance checks for date, place, original upload, edits and whether their caption describes the actual event. A genuine photograph can mislead when assigned to the wrong story. AI-generated material should never be treated as eyewitness evidence.

**Worked calculation:** If 90 of 120 eligible deliveries arrive on time under a stated definition, the rate is 75 percent. Verify what counted as eligible and what time tolerance applied.

**Practice:** Evaluate a social-media image lacking a date and source. List five questions before considering publication.

## 07 — Interviewing, Listening and Sensitive Sources

Prepare by learning the interviewee's role, the timeline already established and the unresolved questions. Begin with neutral open prompts, then ask precise follow-ups about dates, documents, decision authority and the evidence supporting a statement. Avoid leading questions that supply the preferred conclusion. Allow the person to finish and summarize complex answers to check understanding. An effective follow-up can be as simple as asking which record would confirm a stated figure.

A journalist's role is to obtain and test information, not to extract a dramatic line at any cost. When interviewing vulnerable or distressed people, explain your purpose and intended use, respect applicable recording and consent requirements and avoid unnecessary pressure. Distinguish a person's perception from a verifiable fact. If a source refuses an interview, document the request fairly rather than inventing a response or implying consent from silence.

Maintain an interview log recording the source, contact time, attribution agreement, exact key quotations and verification tasks. Review critical transcription against the recording. A technical term, name or number may have been misheard, and an uncorrected transcription error can change the whole claim. If an AI transcription tool is used, verify the output against source audio before quotation.

**Rewrite exercise:** Replace “Why did your agency ignore the warnings?” with “Which warnings were received, on what dates, and what action followed?”

**Practice:** Prepare five neutral interview questions for a fictional public-service interruption.

## 08 — Writing Leads, Headlines and Attribution

A straight-news lead presents the most consequential verified development early. Follow it with the relevant evidence, necessary context, affected groups and response from those responsible. The inverted pyramid is a useful organizing tool for time-sensitive news, although a feature or analysis may legitimately use a different structure. In every genre, the reader should be able to distinguish observed facts, recorded statements, predictions and the reporter's own calculations.

Write a headline only after verifying the central facts of the article. A headline should not exaggerate the result, imply a legal finding that has not occurred or replace an uncertain claim with a categorical accusation. Avoid emotional adjectives and invented scenes. Explain where figures came from and what period they cover. Attribute contested statements to the people or documents making them, without hiding important contrary evidence.

A good paragraph carries one idea with enough context to be understood if quoted elsewhere. Check pronouns, names, chronology and causal language. Do not present correlation as proof of cause. When linking to a primary document, consider whether the URL exposes private details or creates a safety risk. End with the practical state of the story rather than a sensational unsupported claim.

**Fictional supported lead:** “The city announced two temporary water distribution points on Tuesday while officials investigated reports of low water pressure, according to a municipal notice.”

**Practice:** Write a factual headline and 35-word lead that identify their underlying evidence.

## 09 — Editing, Fairness, Corrections and Harm

Editorial review tests both the accuracy of each sentence and the effect of the entire presentation. A story can become misleading through selective omission even if its individual sentences are true. Check exact dates, spellings, units, quotations, legal terminology, captions and the headline. Confirm that serious material allegations were evaluated carefully and that a relevant opportunity to respond was considered. Do not manufacture apparent balance between independently documented facts and unsupported claims.

Corrections should be timely, visible and proportional to the error. Record what was wrong, what changed and when. Do not silently replace a major false statement after it has circulated, or treat a clear factual mistake as merely an alternative viewpoint. For legal questions, safety threats, sensitive identities or allegations that could cause serious harm, seek qualified editorial and legal review appropriate to the jurisdiction before release.

Avoid unnecessary publication of private identifiers, especially for minors, victims and confidential sources. An accurate detail may still be unjustifiably harmful if it adds no significant public value. Do not mislabel synthetic illustrations as documentary images, fabricate quotations, or conceal advertising and sponsorship inside reporting. Editorial independence includes the right to correct a source, sponsor or employer when the evidence requires it.

**Correction model:** “An earlier version incorrectly gave 24 permits. The verified public record lists 14. This article was corrected on [date].”

**Practice:** Prepare a prepublication check covering accuracy, fairness, privacy, conflicts and corrections.

## 10 — Digital Journalism, Multimedia and AI Verification

Digital reporting requires the same verification standards across articles, reels, livestreams and short captions. Each version must preserve the key qualifications and source attribution even when it has less space. Check image and video provenance, original audio, location, date and whether the edit removes important context. Captions should say what the material actually shows, not what a viral repost claims. Accessible captions, readable visual labels and appropriate alternative text can make verified information more useful to different audiences.

AI tools can assist with transcription, organization, translation and editorial checking, but output remains a draft to verify. Do not generate fake eyewitness quotations, create nonexistent sources or publish an AI-produced face as a real person photographed at an event. Inspect translated numbers, names and qualifications carefully. For an image-based claim, check against source media rather than asking a model alone to declare it authentic. Follow applicable publication disclosures and newsroom policy concerning synthetic media and assistance tools.

Audience metrics can reveal how news is received, but maximizing clicks is not the same as serving the public interest. Evaluate completion, accessibility, correction rates and meaningful public utility alongside engagement. Separate editorial articles, paid placement, promotional messages and commentary through clear labeling.

**Practice:** A 12-second video claims to show a disaster. List checks for the original recording, location, date, visible context and independent official information before publication.

## 11 — Complete Reporting Simulation

**Entirely fictional training case.** A city posts a dated notice announcing two temporary bottled-water distribution points while engineers examine complaints of low pressure. A resident posts that the entire network is contaminated. The notice does not make that contamination claim. Your assignment is to write a cautious public-service update without presenting the unsupported social post as confirmed.

First log the notice: issuing body, exact locations, distribution times and contact method. Ask the utility what it knows about the cause, affected neighborhoods and restoration estimates. Ask an appropriate public-health source whether any separate health advisory exists. Record residents' experiences as experiences rather than treating them as proof of contamination. Request primary records. Set a clear decision point for publication: which facts can already be supported and what critical safety information remains unconfirmed?

**Model lead, assuming the fictional notice is verified:** “The city announced two temporary bottled-water distribution points on Tuesday while crews investigate reports of low water pressure in several neighborhoods, according to a municipal notice.” The lead must not imply contamination or invent the cause. A follow-up should give confirmed locations and instructions, explain meaningful uncertainty and include the authority responsible for updated advice.

**Application:** Produce a source-and-claim matrix, a two-paragraph alert, one neutral response request and a correction note in case an address is initially misstated. Evaluate the draft for verification, practical reader value, fair attribution and unnecessary harm.

## 12 — Knowledge Check and Practical Assessment

**Self-test:** (1) What makes a story serve the public interest? (2) How is an allegation different from an established fact? (3) Why are two reposts of a single anonymous claim not independent corroboration? (4) Which details should be recorded when interviewing a source? (5) What makes a percentage misleading? (6) What is a leading question? (7) What belongs in a straight-news lead? (8) How can a true image still mislead? (9) How should a major factual error be corrected? (10) Why must AI-generated speech never be presented as a genuine interview quotation?

**Field assignment:** Write a 500–700-word fictional public-service story or a report relying on properly attributable public records. Include a reporting proposition, claim-and-source matrix, two checks independent of any social-media repost, interview questions or documented response request, a precise headline, a lead, a limitations statement and the final story. Provide a short written note describing how you would handle a later correction.

**Practice rubric:** Verification accuracy (30%), attribution and fairness (20%), structure and clarity (20%), ethics and harm reduction (20%), and correction-ready documentation (10%). Explain each mark and revise unsupported claims. Completing a self-test or reading this manual does not automatically create an official course completion record or certificate.
