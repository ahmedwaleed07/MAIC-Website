## 02 — Course Orientation and Skills Map

Digital journalism gathers, checks and communicates news through online formats, including websites, social networks, newsletters, audio and video. A digital newsroom may publish faster and receive more audience feedback than a print newsroom, but speed does not change the requirement to verify. The same factual assertion must remain supported whether it appears in a full article, a short caption or an alert. This manual explains the production steps and ethical judgments behind an accessible, accountable digital report.

Learners will be able to distinguish a platform post from independently verified reporting; plan a digital reporting workflow; trace user-generated content to its origin; evaluate images, video and data; produce clear web copy and multimedia captions; recognize security, privacy and AI-related risks; and assess performance without confusing engagement with public benefit. The course assumes basic reading and writing ability but no professional newsroom experience.

**Guided study plan:** Orientation, 20 minutes; pages 3–4, 60 minutes; pages 5–6, 70 minutes; pages 7–8, 60 minutes; pages 9–10, 60 minutes; applied verification simulation, page 11, 60 minutes; final practical task, page 12, 90 minutes. Total: 360 minutes. The allocation includes deliberate practice rather than reading only.

**Opening exercise:** Write one factual question that a digital story could help an audience answer. Identify the format most appropriate for the answer and explain which details must remain visible even in a short version.

## 03 — The Digital News Cycle and Public Value

Digital stories can develop in phases: an initial verified alert, a more complete account with primary records, a later explanation and follow-up corrections. Publishing early is useful only when it does not turn uncertainty into fact. Define what is confirmed at each stage and identify the source behind it. A live blog or quickly updated page should show meaningful timestamps and distinguish original statements from subsequent findings.

The audience may encounter a story through a headline, social preview or message notification without opening the full article. Every distributed version therefore needs enough context to avoid a false impression. The most urgent information may be a public-service instruction, while a deeper piece investigates policy or accountability. Readers should know whether information is current, what action it concerns, and where later updates can be verified.

Digital availability can create pressure to imitate trends or repeat viral material. Prioritize usefulness, independent evidence and proportional urgency. A rapidly spreading rumor can be a reason to investigate, but not a reason to report the underlying allegation as established. Record how the newsroom made the publication decision and what remains unresolved.

**Fictional example:** A transit authority announces a temporary route diversion. An initial verified alert may state affected stops and start time; a follow-up can explain cause after appropriate evidence is obtained.

**Practice:** Write a 45-word digital alert and a two-sentence update. Make the level of certainty clear in both.

## 04 — Digital Reporting Workflow and Editorial Roles

A reliable digital workflow separates collection, verification, editing, publication and subsequent monitoring. Assign responsibility for confirming facts, writing, reviewing captions, handling sensitive content and correcting errors. In a small team, one person may hold several roles, but the checks should still be explicit. A content management system can help organize drafts and revisions, yet technology cannot decide whether an allegation is justified.

A report should carry basic metadata: author or responsible desk, update date, headline, relevant location, source references and publication status. Preserve meaningful revision history and original evidence. This allows editors to distinguish what readers saw at one time from what a later correction changed. If a page is updated materially, a visible note may be necessary so readers do not mistake the revised story for the original.

Develop a prepublication checklist appropriate to risk. Ask if every material claim has a source, whether dates and numbers are reconciled, whether a subject has had a fair chance to respond where warranted, whether captions match their media and whether the headline is no stronger than the article. More sensitive material may require specialized legal, safety or ethical review.

**Fictional example:** A web editor receives an article, short video and promotional image. Before publishing, the editor checks that the caption refers to the correct event and that the image is clearly marked illustrative if necessary.

**Practice:** Design a six-stage workflow with named roles and a correction path. Identify one failure point at which a false headline could escape.

## 05 — Verifying Viral Claims and Online Sources

Online popularity is not a measure of truth. When a claim circulates, start by finding the earliest available source and the primary material behind it. Ask who first recorded it, what the uploader could know, whether a dated institutional record exists and whether independent evidence supports the central assertion. Copying and reposting spread a claim but do not independently corroborate it.

For an image, investigate original context, visible landmarks, metadata where available and the earliest traceable appearance. An authentic photograph can be misleading when attached to the wrong city, date or event. For video, check the full recording rather than only the most dramatic excerpt. Audio may be edited or presented out of sequence. Geographical and chronological claims need independent confirmation rather than intuition based on appearance alone.

A digital source log can include URL, access date, original uploader, embedded claims, verification work, retained screenshot or permitted archive and remaining uncertainty. Avoid sharing confidential evidence publicly simply to demonstrate that it was obtained. If a record disappears, distinguish the fact that it was visible from the still-unverified truth of any allegation contained in it.

**Fictional example:** A photo of smoke near a bridge spreads with a claim of a new accident. An archive shows the same image in an unrelated report from two years earlier. The new-event allegation is not established by that photograph.

**Practice:** Prepare a claim-by-claim verification checklist for one fictional viral post containing a date, place, alleged cause and purported eyewitness.

## 06 — User-Generated Media, Consent and Context

User-generated content can provide leads, eyewitness records and local perspectives, but it requires the same verification as professionally gathered material. Identify who created it, when and where, and whether the material was captured firsthand or re-shared. A watermark or profile name alone does not prove authorship or ownership. Do not assume that public accessibility means permission to reproduce a photograph in every context. Consider relevant copyright and privacy rules and seek suitable permissions or legal review where necessary.

Interview contributors carefully about what they saw directly and what they inferred. Ask for the full original file where feasible, without pressuring sources to reveal sensitive personal data. Preserve material context: a short clip of someone shouting may omit what happened immediately before or after. Captions must not convert a witness's interpretation into confirmed fact. Protect minors, victims and people whose location could create a safety risk.

A useful verification record distinguishes authenticity of the file, identity of its creator, claimed time and place, and interpretation of the scene. These are separate questions. A genuine file can show a real crowd but fail to prove why the crowd assembled. When unable to establish essential context, do not use the clip as proof of the broader allegation.

**Fictional example:** A commuter submits a video of an overcrowded platform. The clip may document crowding at that moment, but not the system's average demand or the reason for a service suspension.

**Practice:** Write five questions to ask the creator of a submitted clip. Identify two facts that the video alone cannot establish.

## 07 — Mobile Reporting, Audio and Video Basics

Mobile reporting can combine photos, short video, recorded interviews and notes at the scene. Technical quality supports understanding, but factual context remains the priority. Record stable images of the subject, wide shots that show setting and close details that are relevant without distorting the event. Keep a note of when and where each recording was captured, and use honest framing. A deliberately selective camera angle can create a misleading impression of crowd size or conditions.

For interviews, prioritize intelligible audio and respectful circumstances. Check the microphone before recording, avoid excessive background noise where possible, and confirm relevant permissions. Record identifying statements and locations only when publication would be justified and safe. Do not add soundtrack, dramatic editing or misleading transitions that imply actions happened in a different order. B-roll should illustrate rather than masquerade as footage of an event it does not depict.

A responsible multimedia package states the essential fact in words, gives relevant source attribution, provides accurate captions and contains no synthetic material presented as authentic documentation. Accessibility may include readable subtitles and clear descriptive text. Preserve original footage and maintain a traceable editing log for substantial changes.

**Fictional example:** A reporter films a closed public park gate. The footage shows closure at that time, but a verified council notice is still needed to explain the closure's official reason and duration.

**Practice:** Plan a five-shot sequence with narration for a fictional transit diversion. Mark which claims require documentary checks beyond the images.

## 08 — Writing for Websites, Links and Accessibility

Online readers often encounter fragments: a headline, preview, introductory paragraph or push notification. Each fragment should reflect the actual state of the evidence. Write plain, specific headings and avoid clickbait implying more certainty than the story provides. A clear lead answers the reader's immediate question, with supporting details structured under descriptive subheadings when the article is long. Links to original records can help readers inspect sources, but only when the link is relevant and safe.

Search-friendly writing should improve discoverability without sacrificing accuracy. Use terms that people genuinely seek, not repeated keywords that distort a story's meaning. Avoid introducing accusations or certainty merely to attract clicks. Explain dates, locations and technical expressions. If a page includes material updates, show a meaningful update time and identify substantive corrections. Maintain consistent names and terminology across text, captions and graphics.

Accessibility is part of editorial usefulness. Images may need alternative text that communicates relevant content; audio and video may need captions or transcripts. Avoid conveying critical distinctions only by color. Check contrast, font legibility and whether essential instructions are understandable without playing a video. An accessible explanation should not reveal private identities unnecessarily.

**Fictional example:** A news page titled "Library's Sunday-opening trial: dates, hours and services" is clearer than "You won't believe what happens at the library" and gives a relevant search description.

**Practice:** Draft a web headline, description, two subheadings and an informative image caption. Check each for unsupported implications.

## 09 — Audience Analytics, Data and Editorial Judgment

Digital dashboards can report page views, unique visitors, traffic sources, listening or viewing completion and engagement. These measures describe some aspects of attention; they do not establish factual correctness or public benefit. A story explaining a safety notice may be valuable even with fewer views than entertainment news. Define metrics and denominators before comparing platforms or reporting periods.

Consider how an apparent improvement can arise from a changed tracking method or audience mix. A high click-through rate with very low completion may indicate misleading framing or a mismatch between the headline and the actual story. A short post and a long investigation should not be judged by a single universal engagement target. Combine reach measures with indicators of comprehension, corrections, accessibility and useful follow-up when possible.

Data published as journalism also needs checks. Distinguish an order, person, event or visit; remove duplicates transparently; check missing records and explain sampling limits. A chart should show appropriate units and time periods. Never infer a cause solely from the movement of two numbers together. In analytics-driven editorial reviews, ask what audience behavior the metric actually reflects.

**Fictional example:** An article receives 1,000 visits but only 300 readers reach the end. The observed completion fraction is 30%, assuming the visit and completion counts are defined compatibly.

**Practice:** Define two engagement measures and two balancing quality measures for a public-service news update.

## 10 — Corrections, Security, Privacy and AI

Digital publishing permits quick changes, but silent revision of a material error can mislead readers who saw an earlier version. Provide a clear correction note when a key fact changes, and record the corrected information, time and reason. Identify inaccurate social snippets that may still circulate after the article is fixed. Corrections should not merely protect the newsroom's reputation; they should help readers update their understanding.

Protect source data, login credentials and unreleased materials through appropriate access controls and security practices. Do not publish private addresses or sensitive identifiers without a clear public-interest justification. Images can reveal location or identity through embedded metadata and visible details. In security-sensitive stories, consider both immediate publication risks and how content might be reused. Applicable law and newsroom policies can differ by location.

AI assistance should be bounded by verification. Transcriptions, translations, summaries and generated graphics can contain mistakes or invented details. Validate quotations, names, numbers and implied facts against primary material. Synthetic reconstruction must be clearly differentiated from actual eyewitness documentation. A generated representation should never be presented as a photograph of the reported event. Sensitive input data should not be exposed to unapproved services.

**Fictional example:** An AI transcript incorrectly changes "15 buses" to "50 buses." The correct action is to check the recording, correct the number and prevent the transcription error from reaching the headline.

**Practice:** Produce a correction notice and a three-point AI-assisted reporting policy for a small digital newsroom.

## 11 — Applied Verification Simulation: The Bridge Closure

**All details are hypothetical.** A city transport authority publishes a notice that one lane of a bridge will close for scheduled maintenance from Tuesday morning until Thursday evening. A social account posts an older photo showing smoke near the same bridge and claims a major fire has caused a full indefinite closure. A short video clip repeats the fire allegation but lacks a clear original timestamp. The digital newsroom must decide what it can publish immediately.

Separate the verifiable facts: the authority's stated maintenance work, lane restriction and expected schedule. The notice does not establish a fire, a total closure or an indefinite duration. Inspect the original authority record and request updates if available. Trace the smoke photo to its first publication; a match to an older event would invalidate its use as proof of a current fire. Seek the original video and independent confirmation rather than counting reposts.

**Model verified alert:** "One bridge lane is scheduled to close for maintenance from Tuesday morning until Thursday evening, according to a city transport notice." Add practical route information only if the same notice or a verified companion record supports it. If an emergency develops later, publish a separate clearly timestamped update citing the responsible authority and any independently verified direct evidence.

**Application:** Prepare a claim table identifying source, status and potential harm. Write a 50-word alert, an 80-word web update, a correction note for an inaccurate lane count and a verification plan for the video. Explain why public interest does not justify presenting unsupported disaster allegations as true.

## 12 — Practical Assessment, Questions and Further Reading

**Self-test:** (1) Why does digital speed not eliminate verification? (2) How can a verified photo mislead when reused? (3) What is the difference between an original uploader and a repost account? (4) What should a digital correction identify? (5) Why might two similar engagement rates be incomparable? (6) What could a mobile video fail to prove about an event? (7) How can a caption distort otherwise genuine footage? (8) What makes a web headline accessible and accurate? (9) How should AI transcriptions be checked? (10) What remains unknown in the bridge case?

**Final assignment:** Build a fictional digital-news package using the bridge-closure simulation or another clearly marked classroom case. Deliver a 350–500 word web article, a 50-word alert, a verified evidence table, three relevant interview questions, a multimedia caption, a reader accessibility checklist and a correction note. Include a 120-word editorial explanation of how you avoided amplifying a viral unverified claim.

**Practice rubric:** Accuracy and corroboration 30%; attribution and status labeling 20%; format and accessibility 15%; ethics and privacy 15%; documentation and corrections 10%; audience usefulness 10%. Review the work critically. This practical self-assessment is not a substitute for the academy's official exam or eligibility verification.
