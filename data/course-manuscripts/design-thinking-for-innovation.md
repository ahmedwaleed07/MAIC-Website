## 02 — Innovation Begins with a Human Problem

Design thinking is a collaborative approach to exploring problems, generating possibilities and learning through prototypes and feedback. It can help teams consider user needs alongside technical feasibility and operational value. It is not a rule that every problem must follow five rigid steps in one fixed order, nor does it replace evidence or specialist analysis. The approach works best when teams remain open to changing their assumptions.

Learners will frame a problem, conduct respectful exploratory research, synthesize observations, write a usable problem statement, generate alternatives, select an idea for low-cost testing, construct a prototype, evaluate results and propose an implementation experiment. The final case concerns a fictional community-service booking process.

**Eight-hour guided plan:** Orientation 20 minutes; pages 3–10 at 45 minutes each (360 minutes); case study on page 11 for 60 minutes; reflective portfolio on page 12 for 40 minutes. Total 480 minutes including group exercises. Paper sketches and mock forms are sufficient for practice.

**Starter:** Write down a complaint that a service's booking process is slow. List three possible causes that would need evidence before deciding that the answer is a new mobile app.

## 03 — Understand Context and Stakeholders

Before proposing ideas, understand the service, affected people, incentives and constraints. A community center may have visitors, reception staff, managers, accessibility needs and limited technology capacity. An improvement that helps one group while increasing unnecessary work for another may not be valuable overall. Map who experiences the problem, who owns the process and who might be harmed by a change.

Gather existing evidence. Observe how the current service operates, review support messages, ask staff what causes rework and note variations across busy and quiet times. Avoid assuming every complaint has the same cause. Record known facts, interpretation and open questions separately. A business owner requesting an AI chatbot may be describing a solution rather than an actual root problem.

Identify constraints early without allowing them to prevent exploration: budget, privacy, law, staff training, service standards and technological access. A digital-only intervention may exclude people who rely on telephone or in-person booking. Accessibility and inclusive participation belong in the problem definition.

**Fictional example:** The center receives complaints about confirmation delays. Staff records suggest that a manual approval queue is involved, but the reason for that queue still needs review.

**Practice:** Build a stakeholder map and an assumption/evidence table with at least six entries.

## 04 — Empathy Research and Observation

Empathy in design means trying to understand the user's actual experience rather than projecting the team's preference. Interviews can help people describe goals and frustrations; contextual observation shows what happens during a task. Ask open and nonleading questions, such as “What happened the last time you tried to book a session?” Avoid “Wouldn't an app make this easier?” which pushes respondents toward a favored answer.

Record behaviors and direct quotations accurately, with appropriate consent. Separate a participant's comment from an analyst's interpretation. A small sample may reveal important design opportunities but cannot represent an entire city. Include people with varied access needs and circumstances rather than selecting only the most enthusiastic potential customers.

Use lightweight research artifacts such as an empathy map or journey note, while recognizing that these are analysis tools and not facts in themselves. Revisit observations when a proposed solution conflicts with what people actually reported. Protect sensitive information and avoid publishing identifiable stories without permission.

**Fictional example:** A visitor cannot complete a booking because the confirmation email never arrives. The team investigates mail delivery and alternate confirmation routes rather than assuming the visitor needs a brighter button.

**Practice:** Write five neutral interview questions and a short observation log. Mark each entry as direct evidence or hypothesis.

## 05 — Synthesis, Patterns and Problem Statements

After research, group observations into patterns without losing exceptions. Affinity mapping can cluster related notes about uncertainty, waiting or unclear labels. The analyst must be cautious: three similar complaints suggest a possible problem but do not establish its prevalence. Preserve contradictory observations and explain uncertainties rather than forcing everything into one convenient story.

A useful problem statement identifies the people, need and context, without deciding the technical solution in advance. For example: “Visitors who book community workshops need dependable confirmation because uncertain status leads to repeated inquiries.” This leaves room for changes to staff process, message design or software. “Visitors need a chatbot” is a premature implementation choice.

Ask why a problem exists, but do not treat repeated “why” questions as a substitute for evidence. The cause may involve policy, technical systems, staff capacity or multiple interacting factors. State what the team must validate before investing. Define one intended outcome, such as reducing repeated confirmation inquiries under a clear counting method.

**Fictional example:** Interviews reveal that users interpret “Request received” as a completed reservation. A clearer status message may be a more targeted intervention than rebuilding the entire site.

**Practice:** Write two “How might we…” questions and one measurable problem statement grounded in the observed issue.

## 06 — Ideation, Divergence and Convergence

Ideation explores different ways to address the defined problem. Divergent work encourages variety before narrowing, while convergence uses explicit criteria to select promising directions. A team might consider clearer confirmation text, real-time status, improved staff workflow, an SMS update or a simplified form. Avoid treating the first popular or technically impressive idea as the only possibility.

Brainwriting, sketching and structured rounds can help include quieter participants. Critique ideas against relevant user value, risk, feasibility, cost and evidence. An impact/effort matrix provides a discussion aid, not an objectively correct answer. Assign owners to uncertainties so the group does not confuse enthusiasm with proof.

Balance novelty with practicality. A small text correction can be innovative if it resolves a significant customer problem. A complex application can be wasteful if the actual bottleneck is a policy the app cannot change. Respect the legal and privacy implications of different options.

**Fictional example:** A design session produces twelve ideas. Three are shortlisted: distinguish “pending” from “confirmed,” allow status lookup, and change staff queue assignment. Each will be tested rather than immediately launched.

**Practice:** Generate ten ideas, group them into three categories and compare four against feasibility and inclusion criteria.

## 07 — Prototyping to Learn, Not to Impress

A prototype makes an idea concrete enough to test. It can be a paper screen, service script, clickable interface, sample email or role-played customer journey. Choose fidelity based on the uncertainty. If the team needs to know whether users understand a status message, a text card may be enough; a fully developed platform is unnecessary.

Define what is simulated. A prototype that displays “Confirmed” after a button click must not be presented as an actual booking. Real reservation decisions require verified system data and authorized processes. Mark fictional records and keep personal information out of informal testing artifacts. Build positive and negative states, including delayed approval and failed requests.

Set a learning objective before drawing. Write a hypothesis, expected user behavior and observation method. Keep experiments manageable. Prototype materials should be easily revised when participants reveal misunderstanding.

**Fictional example:** Two printed confirmation cards differ only in wording. One says “We received your request,” while the other makes the pending status and next action explicit. The team tests comprehension of both.

**Practice:** Create two low-fidelity prototypes and a three-question test script without leading language.

## 08 — Test, Observe and Interpret Results

Testing asks whether a prototype solves the intended problem for relevant people. Give participants realistic tasks and observe without telling them how to succeed. Record completion, errors, time, questions and participant explanations. Separate unaided outcomes from those achieved with moderator help, and do not treat user preference alone as proof of better service.

A test may be qualitative or quantitative. A handful of interviews may expose usability defects; estimates of population-wide effects require suitable samples and methods. Avoid changing multiple variables and then attributing an improvement to one visual feature. Report what was actually observed and state limits.

Interpret failure constructively. If people misunderstand a status label, revise it and retest. If participants understand the card but still receive confirmations late, the root problem may be operational, not interface-related. A good test is one that changes the team's understanding of the problem.

**Illustrative arithmetic:** Six of eight fictional testers correctly identify that a reservation is pending. Observed comprehension is 6/8 = 75% for this small group, not a population statistic.

**Practice:** Create a results table and one decision for each of two possible outcomes.

## 09 — Co-Creation, Ethics and Organizational Constraints

Innovation teams need diverse perspectives and clear roles. Co-creation can involve users, staff, designers and technical specialists in evaluating alternatives. Participation should be accessible and transparent about decisions already fixed by law or budget. Inviting people to a workshop does not imply consent to store or publicize all their comments.

Consider effects on less visible groups. A solution requiring a smartphone may fail visitors who prefer a phone call or need language support. Staff may face increased workload if an automation fails. Design options should consider privacy, security, accessibility, training and long-term maintenance as well as ease of implementation.

Document responsibility and escalation. For a sensitive service, an apparently helpful AI answer could invent a confirmation or misstate eligibility. Human oversight and verified transactional systems remain important. Ethical review is not a decorative stage; it can change the product or process design.

**Fictional example:** The center decides to offer both a web status lookup and a staff-assisted phone option, then measures how each performs.

**Practice:** Make a six-risk table including affected group, mitigation and owner.

## 10 — Pilot, Implementation and Continual Learning

A promising prototype becomes an intervention only after planning resources and operational controls. Define scope, success conditions, baseline, budget, staff ownership, deployment steps and rollback. Pilot with a limited group when appropriate, while preserving existing service access. Do not declare a measurable improvement solely because a prototype received favorable comments.

Select indicators linked to user value: confirmation misunderstandings, repeated support inquiries, time from request to accurate status and unresolved errors. Include balancing measures such as staff workload, privacy incidents and accessibility barriers. Data collection methods and denominators should be stable across baseline and follow-up. Other changes can influence results, so avoid unjustified causal claims.

A pilot may reveal that the solution requires a change to policy or an external supplier. Record dependencies and prioritize safety and legality. Keep a traceable change log and communicate which claims are verified, estimated or still unresolved.

**Fictional example:** A clearer email status is deployed to a small test group, with inquiry rates recorded over two comparable periods. A decline suggests promise but needs evaluation of other factors.

**Practice:** Write a four-week pilot plan with five metrics, risks, owners and stop conditions.

## 11 — Worked Case: Improving Workshop Booking Confirmation

**Entirely fictional design challenge.** A community center accepts requests for limited workshop seats. Customers often confuse “Request received” with confirmed enrollment. Staff approval sometimes takes longer than expected. The team wants fewer repeated inquiries and more reliable user understanding without making a false booking promise.

Start with a stakeholder map. Interview four invented persona types only as planning scenarios; real interviews must be gathered separately. Draft a neutral research script. Create a journey showing submitted, pending, approved, waitlisted and rejected states. Define a problem statement focused on trustworthy status communication.

Develop three options: clearer message copy, status lookup and staff-process change. Make two paper prototypes of a pending confirmation. Run a fictional comprehension test with eight participants, six of whom understand the pending state without help, for an observed rate of 75%. Preserve this limited sample framing. Compare user clarity and operational burden before choosing a pilot.

**Deliverables:** Evidence/assumption table, one problem statement, ten ideas, decision matrix, two prototypes, small test plan, results table, accessible alternative route and four-week pilot design.

**Reflection:** Explain why an attractive new mobile app may fail if staff approval continues to be slow and status semantics remain unclear.

## 12 — Design Thinking Portfolio and References

**Self-test:** (1) Why begin with a human problem? (2) How does observation differ from a hypothesis? (3) What makes an interview question leading? (4) Why should the problem statement avoid a preselected solution? (5) What is divergence? (6) What makes a prototype appropriately low fidelity? (7) Why should a small user test not be generalized? (8) How can co-creation support inclusion? (9) What belongs in a responsible pilot? (10) Why should a solution's operational burden be evaluated?

**Final assignment:** Work through a fictional service-improvement challenge: produce a brief, stakeholder map, three evidence gaps, interview questions, observation synthesis, two “How might we” questions, ten design ideas, two low-cost prototypes, a test plan and a pilot recommendation. Explain where further verification or expert input is required.

**Practice rubric:** Research integrity 20%; problem framing 20%; diverse and feasible ideas 15%; prototype and testing 20%; inclusion and ethics 15%; implementation learning 10%. Classroom exercises are not a credential, guarantee of innovation or official MAIC assessment.
