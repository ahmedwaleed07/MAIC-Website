## 02 — Designing Rules, Choices and Experiences

Game design organizes rules, goals, player actions, feedback and challenge into a coherent experience. A game can be a board game, a physical activity or a digital simulation; all require understandable interactions and meaningful decisions. Beautiful art alone cannot compensate for a rule system that makes the objective impossible or rewards the wrong behavior.

Learners should identify a target player, state a core experience, construct a basic gameplay loop, describe mechanics and dynamics, create a small rulebook, balance difficulty and rewards, sketch level progression, prototype interactions, observe playtests and revise a design using evidence. The final exercise is an original paper-or-digital microgame, not a remake of a commercially protected title.

**Eight-hour guided plan:** Introduction 20 minutes; eight sections on pages 3–10 at 45 minutes each (360 minutes); game prototype on page 11 for 60 minutes; portfolio review on page 12 for 40 minutes. Total 480 minutes, including testing. Paper tokens, index cards and dice are sufficient; no particular game engine is required.

**Starter:** Invent a one-minute challenge in which a player must deliver supplies across three spaces. Write the goal, three permitted actions and one condition for failure.

## 03 — Player Needs, Design Pillars and the Brief

A game brief names audience, platform, play duration, intended emotion, accessibility constraints and central decision. A relaxing puzzle for short sessions differs from a cooperative strategy game for longer group play. Avoid assuming all players with similar ages or regions have identical preferences. Research users through conversations and observed play, not stereotypes.

Define two or three design pillars that guide choices. For a delivery puzzle, these could be clear planning, low-pressure experimentation and visible feedback. Every proposed feature should reinforce one pillar or serve an essential function. A large feature list without prioritization can make the core game difficult to understand and produce.

Set scope according to available development skills and time. A paper prototype can test whether decisions are fun before code and final art. Document unknowns, such as whether players understand the goal or perceive the challenge as fair. These are hypotheses for playtests, not already established truths.

**Fictional example:** A class wants a cooperative game about moving relief supplies safely. The brief limits the board to nine spaces and three resource types, avoiding an unmanageable simulation of a real disaster.

**Practice:** Write a one-page brief with player hypothesis, design pillars, constraints and two explicit test questions.

## 04 — Mechanics, Dynamics and Core Gameplay Loop

Mechanics are the rules and permitted actions: move, collect, trade, place or spend a resource. Dynamics describe behavior that emerges when players interact with those mechanics, such as cooperation, risk-taking or competition. The core loop is a repeatable sequence connecting decisions to feedback and consequences. A loop should support the game's objective rather than generate repetitive actions without choice.

Explain inputs, outputs and resources precisely. If players gain one energy token per turn and travel costs two tokens, they need to plan when to save and when to move. A rule may be mathematically valid yet create boring or impossible choices. Test edge cases, such as having zero resources, reaching the final space or two players attempting the same action.

Feedback should reveal important changes. A player who spends a token must see what happened and understand why. Avoid hiding essential rules until after a failure unless discovery is an intentional and fair part of the design.

**Worked example:** A fictional player begins with five energy tokens, spends two on travel and one on a tool. Two tokens remain. The arithmetic must agree with the displayed resource count.

**Practice:** Draw a five-step loop and specify one meaningful choice, one feedback event and one failure recovery path.

## 05 — Goals, Difficulty, Balance and Progression

Players need to know what success means and how actions contribute to it. A clear win condition can be reaching a location with enough supplies before a defined turn limit; loss conditions should be stated without unnecessary ambiguity. Challenge should emerge from decisions, not unpredictable instructions or a broken interface.

Balance involves comparing available choices and the costs and benefits they create. A strong strategy may still be fair if others can counter it; a dominant strategy removes meaningful decisions. Difficulty should reflect player learning and available information. Early levels can introduce one mechanic at a time, while later stages combine known ideas in new ways.

Avoid claiming balance from one designer's impression. Measure play time, successful strategies, failure causes, abandoned sessions and participants' explanations. Test whether unexpected outcomes result from skill, chance or unclear rules. A design may deliberately use randomness, but players should understand its role and constraints.

**Fictional example:** The fastest route has a high energy cost; a slower route allows collection of supplies. Playtesting evaluates whether both routes remain viable for different situations.

**Practice:** Create three difficulty steps and a balance table comparing two strategies under the same starting resources.

## 06 — Level Design, Space and Player Guidance

Level design shapes choices through space, obstacles, resources and information. A well-designed level teaches through safe experimentation and feedback, not only lengthy instructions. A path's visibility and alternative routes influence exploration. In a digital game, camera placement and input affordances are part of navigation; in a board game, tile layout and symbols communicate options.

Plan an introductory stage with a simple objective, a practice action and an understandable result. Later stages can add an obstacle, limited time or a resource tradeoff. Avoid introducing several new mechanics without explanation. Check whether players can reach every required area with the available moves; a hidden dead end can create an unintended impossible state.

Use spatial clarity. Important objects should be distinguishable without relying only on color. A level can be surprising while remaining understandable. Maintain accessibility for text, controls, navigation and audio alternatives as relevant to the target platform.

**Fictional example:** A nine-tile board first teaches how to cross a bridge, then introduces a blocked tile requiring an alternate route. The instructions are tested with new players.

**Practice:** Sketch a three-level progression and label the skill learned, decision tested and possible frustration at each level.

## 07 — Prototyping and Iterative Playtesting

A prototype tests risky design assumptions with minimal cost. Paper pieces can represent characters, resources and terrain. A digital prototype may add input and timing behavior, but it need not contain final art. Define the most important question: Can players understand the goal and make meaningful choices? Test that before polishing textures.

Give participants the goal without instructing every move. Observe confusion, incorrect assumptions, time to start and decisions made. Separate unassisted completion from completion after moderator guidance. Record direct observations and player comments, then decide which rules to revise. Five classmates may reveal several concrete problems but cannot establish a precise success rate for all possible players.

Iterate deliberately. Change one major rule where feasible and compare the resulting experience. A successful playtest is not one in which no one complains; it is one that produces useful evidence about enjoyment, clarity and mechanics. Preserve versioned rulebooks so changes remain traceable.

**Fictional example:** Four testers repeatedly miss a collection point. The team adds a clearer icon and tests again instead of blaming players for not reading instructions.

**Practice:** Draft three playtest tasks, an observation sheet and a one-page revision record.

## 08 — Feedback, Accessibility and Ethical Game Systems

A usable game gives feedback about available actions, current state, errors and achievements. Visual, audio and haptic cues can complement each other, but important status should not rely solely on a color difference. Controls should be understandable and responsive. Where supported, remapping, readable subtitles and difficulty options can improve access.

Ethical design includes avoiding deception about probabilities or virtual rewards. Randomized systems should be transparent in relevant contexts and meet applicable law and platform rules. Designers should not use manipulative monetization to make a game difficult until a player spends money. For children, marketing and safety responsibilities need particular attention.

Fairness also involves social interaction. Cooperative games require clear rules around shared objectives and communication. Competitive systems should address griefing, harassment and the risk of excluding particular players. A game can intentionally include difficulty and surprise without misleading players about basic consequences.

**Fictional example:** A player has no energy to move. The game shows why the move is unavailable and offers a rule-supported way to recover, rather than silently ignoring input.

**Practice:** Audit five game states for understandable feedback, accessibility and ethical information.

## 09 — Narrative, Art, Sound and Originality

Narrative can provide meaning to mechanics through characters, setting and player choice. Story should be consistent with what players actually do. A game about careful delivery should not reward reckless resource waste without explaining why. Art communicates spaces, objects and status; a strong visual identity may use a limited palette and coherent icon language.

Sound reinforces actions, time pressure and atmosphere but should not obscure essential instructions. Captions or non-audio alternatives help convey important events. Avoid using copyrighted music, characters, environments or logos from existing games without appropriate rights. “Inspired by a genre” does not authorize copying protected expression.

Prototype original assets proportionate to the scope. A clear colored tile and readable action label can be more useful than elaborate character artwork that hides the game rules. A future production may develop a full art bible after the mechanics are tested.

**Fictional example:** The class delivery game uses invented symbols for energy, route safety and cargo. The same symbol means the same thing throughout the game.

**Practice:** Write an art direction sheet, three original icons and a short sound/rights checklist.

## 10 — Metrics, Release Quality and Responsible Scope

A game release requires more than a playable first run. Verify instructions, input behavior, saving and restarting, level completion, accessibility, performance and unexpected states. Test what happens if a player exits mid-task or receives an impossible resource configuration. A functional end condition should be reliably detected.

Design metrics require clear definitions. Completion rate, level restarts, session duration and player feedback may help identify problems. None alone proves enjoyment or long-term value. Observe behavior alongside numbers and protect private player information. Maintain version notes and a process to correct broken rules.

Plan scope realistically. Extra modes and complex progression may be attractive but can delay the core game. Build a stable small experience first, evaluate the loop and expand only when the existing systems support it. Communicate to reviewers which parts are functional and which are simulated.

**Fictional example:** The paper game appears balanced, but a later rule change permits infinite energy collection. The issue is logged, corrected and retested before the next distribution.

**Practice:** Create a release checklist and five edge-case tests with expected results.

## 11 — Worked Case: The Nine-Tile Supply Puzzle

**Entirely fictional original game.** A player starts at tile 1 on a nine-tile map and must deliver a package to tile 9 within six turns. The player has five energy tokens; a normal move costs one, and crossing a special bridge costs two. An optional collection tile grants one additional token. The rules and map are classroom material, not an existing commercial game.

Draw the nine-tile board with three alternative paths. Define when energy is spent and whether moves may be reversed. Check that a route to the goal is possible under the intended resource limit. Playtest two strategies and identify a point where the player makes a meaningful choice. Make sure all numbers in the rulebook match the tokens actually used.

**Example:** If a player spends two energy crossing the bridge and one on a later move, the starting five becomes two before collecting new energy. That calculation alone does not determine whether the goal is reachable: the remaining route and rules must also be checked.

Create an introductory level explaining basic movement, a second level with the bridge tradeoff and a third stage requiring a plan. Let a new player attempt the game without being shown the correct route. Record confusion and one revision based on evidence.

**Deliverables:** Brief, rulebook, board, core loop, level progression, balancing notes, playtest observations and versioned improvement.

## 12 — Practice Questions and Game Design Portfolio

**Self-test:** (1) What is a game mechanic? (2) How do dynamics differ from mechanics? (3) What defines a core loop? (4) Why do explicit win and loss conditions matter? (5) What is a dominant strategy problem? (6) How can level layouts teach controls? (7) What should a playtest observer record? (8) Why does game accessibility need more than color cues? (9) How can sound and story support mechanics? (10) Why should release testing include unusual player states?

**Final project:** Design an original small game that can be completed in a short session. Provide a brief, player hypothesis, three design pillars, complete rules, board or wireframe, mechanic and reward descriptions, a three-level progression, five meaningful test cases, playtest notes and a revised edition. State where randomness exists and what participants are expected to understand.

**Practice rubric:** Core goal and loop 20%; meaningful decisions and balance 20%; levels and clarity 20%; playtesting 15%; inclusive ethical feedback 15%; versioning and originality 10%. Practice exercises do not constitute a formal MAIC exam or game-industry certification.
