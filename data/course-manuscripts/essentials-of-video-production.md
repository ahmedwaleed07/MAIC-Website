## 02 — Orientation: Plan, Capture, Edit and Deliver

Video production transforms a communication purpose into a sequence of images and sounds that an audience can understand. A strong production is not defined by the price of its camera. It depends on a clear brief, thoughtful sequencing, stable and intelligible footage, recorded sound, respectful treatment of contributors, coherent editing and a delivery format suited to the viewer. Technical choices are useful when they support meaning rather than distract from it.

After completing this course, learners should be able to write a production brief, outline a short script, prepare a storyboard or shot list, recognize basic composition and exposure controls, design simple lighting, record usable sound, manage continuity, identify consent and safety concerns, edit a simple sequence and check an export against the project's objectives. Exercises may be performed using a suitable smartphone, camera or available editing application.

**Guided study allocation:** Orientation 20 minutes; pages 3–4 60 minutes; pages 5–6 70 minutes; pages 7–8 60 minutes; pages 9–10 60 minutes; practical case page 11 60 minutes; final assignment and review page 12 90 minutes. Total 360 minutes, including hands-on work.

**Starter exercise:** Choose a 45-second educational video idea. Write one sentence identifying the audience, the question the video answers and the action or understanding viewers should gain. Do not select the camera effects before the message.

## 03 — Purpose, Audience and the Production Brief

A production brief should specify the intended audience, communication goal, key message, approximate duration, distribution context, factual sources, resources and deadline. A video explaining a public-service change requires different evidence and pacing from a product demonstration or fictional dramatic scene. Clear purpose shapes what to record and what not to include. The brief should also identify accessibility and language needs, such as subtitles, spoken narration or visual text.

Define a measurable success condition that is related to the objective. For an instructional video, an appropriate criterion might be that a new viewer can correctly describe the required process after watching. For a factual public-service update, accuracy of the time and location may matter more than a high view count. Aesthetic appeal and public usefulness can reinforce one another, but attractive footage cannot compensate for misinformation.

List constraints openly: filming permission, weather, available light, staff, privacy, background noise, equipment, delivery resolution and time. Prioritize essential shots that demonstrate the message. A small team should choose a feasible approach rather than promise a complicated sequence it cannot complete safely or consistently.

**Fictional example:** A community center wants a 45-second video announcing new weekend hours. The brief needs verified times and services, a readable on-screen schedule and a plan for any visitor appearing recognizably in the frame.

**Practice:** Write a one-paragraph brief and a list of five essential facts or shots. Mark each factual statement with its source.

## 04 — Scriptwriting, Structure and Storyboards

A short video usually benefits from an opening that defines its topic, a small number of ordered points and a clear conclusion. Write spoken words for listening rather than for reading: concise sentences, natural cadence and enough time for the viewer to process images and captions. A script may use two columns, one for what is heard and one for what appears on screen. A storyboard can use sketches or simple written frames to show the proposed sequence.

Plan transitions based on meaning. A change in location, direction or time needs a clear visual or verbal bridge; otherwise viewers may infer a relationship that does not exist. Distinguish fictional reenactments, illustrative B-roll and documentary footage. A staged scene must never be presented as authentic footage of a real reported event. Confirm that graphics and voiceover describe what the available evidence actually supports.

A shot list turns the script into a practical checklist: scene, location, framing, action, sound, required permission and priority. Create alternatives for key shots in case equipment or weather prevents the original plan. A brief rehearsal can reveal whether the planned narration is too long or a demonstration needs another camera angle.

**Fictional example:** For a community center video, show the exterior sign, the verified schedule, the entrance route, a staff explanation and a closing information screen. Do not imply services are operating if the footage is only an illustrative rehearsal.

**Practice:** Make a six-shot storyboard with one sentence of narration or caption per frame. Note what each shot proves or illustrates.

## 05 — Camera Framing, Composition and Continuity

Framing determines which facts viewers can observe and what remains outside the picture. Wide shots establish location and scale; medium shots show action and relationships; close-ups direct attention to details. Choose shots to answer the communication question, not solely for visual variety. Maintain consistent orientation and camera direction when clarity matters, especially across a sequence showing movement. Unexplained changes in perspective can make the geography confusing.

Composition can use headroom, lead room, balanced backgrounds and clear separation between subject and surroundings. A clean composition is not automatically an objective representation of a whole event. A tight crop can exclude a nearby queue or make a small group appear large. When producing factual material, avoid visual choices that materially misrepresent scale, cause or chronology.

Continuity involves relationships between shots: which hand holds an object, where a person stands, the direction of movement, lighting, wardrobe and the order of actions. Errors may be distracting in fiction and factually misleading in news. Capture reference frames and notes when a scene takes several takes. Use stable support or controlled handheld movement appropriate to the situation; shaky movement may hide the information the viewer needs.

**Fictional example:** A presenter demonstrates a door-access procedure. If the card changes hands between cuts without an actual transfer, the process becomes harder to follow.

**Practice:** Record a wide, medium and close shot of the same safe activity. Write which distinct information each shot communicates.

## 06 — Exposure, Focus, White Balance and Motion

Exposure controls how bright or dark recorded image information appears. Bright scenes may lose detail in highlights, while underexposed footage may conceal details in shadow. Monitor the subject rather than judging solely by a bright display. Camera systems vary, but shutter, aperture and sensor gain or ISO are common exposure influences. Changing one can affect motion rendering, depth of field or visible noise. Test rather than memorizing one setting for all situations.

Focus directs attention and preserves relevant detail. Autofocus can be useful when subjects move, but may shift to a distracting foreground object. Verify critical focus before and after a take. White balance should produce consistent and appropriate color under the scene's light. Mixed light sources can create unnatural color changes between shots; a consistent setting or thoughtful correction improves continuity.

Frame rate and shutter choices influence the appearance of movement. Select them for the desired motion and the production's delivery context, considering local lighting and equipment limitations. Do not imply that changing a frame rate can create authentic slow motion from footage that was not captured with adequate information. Capture a short test clip, review it at realistic playback size and correct problems before the full shoot.

**Fictional example:** A bright window behind an interviewee turns the face into a dark silhouette. Repositioning the subject or adding appropriate fill light may be more effective than raising gain indiscriminately.

**Practice:** Record the same stationary subject in two lighting positions. Compare shadow detail, color and focus, and explain which version serves clarity.

## 07 — Lighting for Clarity and Mood

Lighting determines what viewers can see and helps establish atmosphere. The key light provides the main illumination; fill reduces selected shadows; separation or background light can help the subject stand out. These are functional roles, not equipment purchase requirements. A window, practical lamp or reflected light can sometimes perform them effectively. The appropriate setup depends on the scene, available power, time and safety.

Observe the direction, quality and color of existing light before placing a camera. Soft light tends to produce broad shadow transitions; hard light makes more defined edges. A bright background can distract from or obscure a face. Matching color temperature between shots simplifies editing, although mixed light may be an intentional visual choice if it remains truthful to the scene. Avoid lighting or color manipulation that changes an informational image into a misleading representation.

Test for flicker, reflections and unwanted shadows. Protect people from heat, unstable stands, exposed cables and electrical hazards. Ask whether a lighting choice is safe for participants and the location. A visually attractive arrangement is not successful if it prevents the subject from working normally or compromises the accuracy of the production.

**Fictional example:** A tutor recording an instructional demonstration places a light to illuminate both hands and equipment instead of lighting only the tutor's face.

**Practice:** Draw a simple lighting plan for a one-person interview with a nearby window. Label the main light, possible fill and background risks.

## 08 — Recording Usable Sound

Audio often determines whether a viewer can understand a video at all. A clear image cannot compensate for missing instructions or unintelligible speech. Select an appropriate microphone placement, monitor distance and listen for competing noise, echo and handling sounds. Test a short recording at the actual location, then review it with headphones. Meter displays can help identify excessive levels, but listening remains essential.

Record a few seconds of the room's ambient sound when useful for editing, but do not use it to fabricate a false acoustic event. Check for clipping on loud words, extremely low levels and microphone rubbing. If the presenter moves, make sure the microphone continues capturing usable speech. Obtain consent where required for recordings and consider whether bystanders' private conversations should be excluded from publication.

Narration, music and sound effects must serve the message. A soundtrack should not drown out instructions or falsely suggest danger in factual material. Use only audio you have permission to include; publicly accessible songs are not automatically licensed for commercial or educational reuse. Add subtitles or a transcript when appropriate and review them for names, numerical facts and wording.

**Fictional example:** A short safety instruction recorded beside a noisy generator is hard to understand. Moving to a quieter space or using a better microphone position can improve comprehension more than adding graphic effects.

**Practice:** Record two 20-second instructions at different microphone distances. Evaluate clarity, background noise and any clipped words.

## 09 — Directing, Participants, Safety and Production Records

A production director coordinates the purpose of a scene, what participants need to do, camera coverage, sound, timing and safe working conditions. Clear communication reduces repeated takes and avoidable mistakes. Give performers or presenters one actionable instruction at a time, and rehearse the movement when feasible. For an interview, use neutral prompts and do not ask participants to present invented events as personal experience.

Consent and location permissions matter. Determine whether participants have been informed about the intended use of their image or voice and whether recording is permissible in the setting. Protect minors and vulnerable individuals with extra care and follow applicable law and organizational requirements. Do not place anyone in unsafe traffic, unstable locations or hazardous equipment zones for a more dramatic shot. The person responsible for safety can stop a shoot even when it disrupts a schedule.

Keep production records: shot identifiers, date, location, take quality, source of any factual graphics, participant permissions and notes about potential continuity problems. Record which scenes are documentary and which are staged or illustrative. Maintain backups appropriate to the production's sensitivity and avoid exposing raw footage containing private information to unauthorized people.

**Fictional example:** A presenter records instructions in an active workshop. The crew moves the setup away from operating machinery rather than ask the presenter to stand in a danger zone for better framing.

**Practice:** Prepare a one-page call sheet with role assignments, essential shots, safety checks, consent checks and a backup plan.

## 10 — Editing, Captions, Graphics and Delivery Quality

Editing chooses the order and duration of shots to serve the communication goal. Begin with organizing footage by scene and verifying its contents; then assemble a rough cut, assess the logic, improve sound and picture consistency, add necessary titles and captions, and export a review version. Cutting faster is not always clearer. Leave enough time for viewers to read important text and recognize transitions. If continuity is broken, replace the confusing cut or show an honest bridging shot.

Text graphics must be legible, accurate and consistent with the visual identity. Use readable placement, sufficient contrast and stable terminology. Subtitles are part of the content and need proofreading, especially for names and numbers. Do not present illustrations, stock footage or generated visuals as genuine documentation of a specific real incident. Check music and image permissions and record any necessary credit.

The export should match the intended delivery platform's requirements without claiming that one resolution or codec is universally correct. Test actual playback, audio levels, captions, crop and aspect ratio on relevant devices. Retain an editable project and original assets where appropriate. A final quality pass checks factual statements, editorial ethics, technical playback and whether the result achieves its stated brief.

**Fictional example:** A 45-second schedule announcement has a correct voiceover but a graphic displaying the wrong date. The export must be corrected before release, even when its images are attractive.

**Practice:** Create a rough-cut checklist and a separate final-export checklist. Include text accuracy, sound clarity, continuity, consent and playback.

## 11 — Applied Project: A Community Center Schedule Video

**Entirely fictional exercise.** A local community center issues a dated notice that it will extend Saturday opening by two hours for a six-week trial. The notice names the new closing time and available services but does not promise a permanent change. The production brief is to make a 45-second information video that helps visitors understand the trial without implying that it has already become permanent.

Plan six shots: exterior identifying sign; presenter introducing the announcement; a schedule graphic verified against the notice; a neutral view of the entrance; a relevant service demonstration clearly marked as illustrative if staged; and a closing source-and-contact screen. Record two alternate versions of the presenter line so it can be understood clearly. Obtain any required permissions, avoid identifiable visitors when unnecessary and keep the composition consistent across shots.

**Model narration:** "The community center has announced a six-week trial of extended Saturday hours beginning next month. The updated schedule and available services are listed in the center's dated notice." Add the exact hours only when confirmed from the practice source. Do not add invented budget figures, visitor feedback or claims that the trial will become permanent. A good edit preserves the difference between announced and completed action.

**Application:** Submit a brief, script, shot list, storyboard, sound plan, one-page risk assessment and timed rough-cut outline. Explain how you would correct the published video if the schedule graphic displayed an inaccurate closing time.

## 12 — Self-Test, Production Assignment and Rubric

**Knowledge check:** (1) What should a production brief contain? (2) How is a shot list different from a script? (3) What is the purpose of an establishing shot? (4) Which setting helps prevent inconsistent color between takes? (5) What can happen if a window is much brighter than an interview subject? (6) Why must speech be monitored at the recording location? (7) What is continuity? (8) Why should illustrative footage be labeled? (9) What must be checked before using background music? (10) Which facts must be verified before exporting the community-center schedule video?

**Practical assignment:** Plan and, if safely possible, produce a 45–60-second informational video using the fictional community-center case or another clearly marked classroom scenario. Include a one-page brief, two-column script, six-frame storyboard, annotated shot list, audio test notes, permission and safety checklist, rough cut and export checklist. An approved recording environment and available equipment may be used; learners who cannot film should create a detailed production plan with annotated sample frames rather than invent completed footage.

**Practice rubric:** Brief and audience fit 15%; factual and ethical accuracy 20%; structure and story clarity 20%; visual and sound planning 20%; continuity and accessibility 15%; delivery and self-correction 10%. Identify limitations and revise accordingly. This is a self-learning exercise, not an official pass result or an automatically issued certificate.
