## 02 — Scope, Outcomes and Twelve-Hour Learning Route

Social media marketing is the planned use of social channels to inform, build relationships and encourage measurable actions. A campaign is a business process rather than a collection of decorative posts: it begins with a goal, an audience, an offer, evidence of performance and an adjustment loop. Organic distribution, partnerships, community management and paid placements can work together, but each serves a different role. Activity counts alone cannot establish business impact.

Learners should be able to write a measurable objective, define audience segments without stereotyping, choose appropriate channels, develop messaging and a content calendar, prepare rights-cleared creative material, plan a paid-media test, document creator disclosures, respond to communities responsibly, define marketing metrics and interpret a small campaign experiment. They should understand privacy and measurement limitations rather than promise a platform will deliver a guaranteed outcome.

**Guided study allocation:** Orientation, 30 minutes; eight core sections on pages 3–10 at 60 minutes each, 480 minutes; the applied campaign case on page 11, 120 minutes; and the final assignment with reflection on page 12, 90 minutes. Total: 720 minutes, or 12 hours. Activities are part of the stated time. An official MAIC exam is separate from the practice questions in this manual.

**Opening exercise:** Choose a hypothetical business service, such as website design. Describe the desired customer action, the evidence the business could measure and one risk of using engagement counts as proof of sales.

## 03 — Objectives, Positioning and Customer Journey

Begin with a real organizational objective. Awareness may help people discover an offering; consideration supports comparison and understanding; conversion asks for an identifiable action; retention encourages existing customers to return or recommend. The appropriate objective depends on the business model, capacity and customer needs. A seller seeking qualified service inquiries should not judge success solely by the number of likes on a post.

Define the desired behavior and timeframe. A measurable statement might be: generate 40 qualified inquiries through a clear website form in the next 30 days, within an agreed advertising budget. Identify what counts as qualified, how the inquiries will be recorded and who can respond. A goal is not automatically feasible: compare the target with the organization's sales capacity, current evidence and operating constraints.

Positioning explains why the offering matters to a particular audience. Describe the problem solved, credible value, relevant differentiators and limits. Claims such as "guaranteed results" or "best in the world" require appropriate evidence and can mislead. Map the journey from discovery through evaluation, inquiry, purchase and continued service. Each stage may require different content: a short introduction, an evidence-based explanation, a practical case, clear pricing conditions or a helpful follow-up.

**Fictional example:** A website design agency wants inquiries from restaurant owners. An awareness clip can show mobile menus; a consideration article can explain speed and maintenance; the inquiry page should state how to request a quote without inventing guaranteed revenue growth.

**Practice:** Draft one measurable objective and three pieces of evidence that would show progress along the customer journey.

## 04 — Research, Segmentation and Channel Choice

A target audience is not simply everybody in a country. Segment by needs, context, feasible service area, purchasing roles, accessibility requirements and evidence of interest. For B2B services, an individual may discover a solution while another person approves spending. For consumer products, price, timing and trust may dominate. Avoid assuming that age, language or location automatically predicts all behavior; use research and revise assumptions.

Start with interviews, customer service questions, site search terms, relevant market reports and ethically obtained feedback. Competitor pages can show visible messaging and offers, but social metrics do not prove a rival's profit. Create a basic audience persona with a problem, preferred information, likely objections and an achievable action. Use personas as hypotheses rather than stereotypes or evidence of an actual individual.

Choose channels according to communication fit and resources. Short video can demonstrate a visible process, professional networks may suit detailed B2B explanations, and search-accessible articles can answer recurring questions. A business need not publish on every platform. Consider the effort needed to respond, the available creative formats and whether the intended audience can access the service. Treat algorithm and advertising interface features as changeable; verify current policies before launching.

**Fictional example:** A supplier of industrial filtration equipment may find a technical explanatory post and sales follow-up more useful than high-volume entertainment clips, despite lower reach.

**Practice:** Compare two hypothetical channels for a defined audience. State the objective, content type, measurement and operational constraint of each.

## 05 — Messaging, Content Pillars and Creative Production

Content pillars organize recurring subject areas around audience value. A professional-services brand might use education, evidence-based demonstrations, customer questions and service updates. Pillars should not turn every message into the same promotion. The best creative answers a reader's genuine question or explains a verifiable benefit. A useful brief names the audience, message, factual sources, intended action, tone, format and language.

The first seconds of a short video should establish the topic without making unsupported promises. Use accurate images, legible type and clear audio. Visual identity can remain consistent across platforms while layouts adapt to different aspect ratios. Captions and alternative text may make content more accessible. Check that the published image depicts the actual product or is clearly illustrative. Do not generate a fictional person and describe that individual as a real customer testimonial.

Write claims with evidence boundaries. "Our team can build a responsive site" is a service statement if true; "your sales will triple" is a performance prediction requiring meaningful substantiation. A case study should distinguish actual results from models or hypothetical examples. Calls to action should match the destination: if an audience is told it can book a consultation, the linked page should provide a functioning, understandable route to do so.

**Example:** Pillar: customer education. Post: "Five details to prepare before requesting a restaurant website quote." Include menu sections, branding assets, booking needs, update ownership and contact process.

**Practice:** Draft one carousel outline, one 30-second video script and one answer to a common customer question. Flag every statement needing factual support.

## 06 — Content Calendar and Community Operations

A content calendar turns a strategy into consistent, achievable work. For each item, record planned date, audience segment, objective, content pillar, format, responsible editor, evidence source, rights status, call to action and approval stage. A calendar is an operational control rather than a guarantee of engagement. Publish fewer accurate pieces if a team cannot properly review or respond to a larger volume.

Community management includes answering genuine questions, routing service problems, moderating abuse and identifying useful customer feedback. Define response responsibilities and escalation paths. A complaint about a failed order should not receive an automatic advertising slogan. Where private information is needed to resolve a case, move the conversation to an appropriate secure channel rather than requesting account details publicly.

Distinguish customer care from reputation manipulation. Do not invent reviews, purchase fake engagements or pressure users to repeat favorable stories. Moderation rules should be clear and applied consistently; negative feedback alone is not evidence of misconduct. For sensitive or potentially defamatory allegations, preserve the available record and use a suitable review process. Staff should know what they may promise about price, service and response time.

**Fictional example:** An agency plans three weekly posts, one Q&A session and daily reply checks. The operations team has enough capacity to prepare and fact-check the content but not to run a daily livestream; the plan is adjusted rather than pretending capacity is unlimited.

**Practice:** Create a seven-day calendar with four items, owners, review states and response duties. Write a polite answer to a customer asking for pricing details not yet confirmed.

## 07 — Paid Promotion, Creative Tests and Budget Controls

Paid social advertising buys opportunities to reach selected audiences; it does not guarantee sales. Begin with an objective, a measured conversion path, a budget limit and a clear destination page. Ensure the advertised offer, landing page and actual service terms agree. Review the current platform's rules for targeting, prohibited claims, disclosures and sensitive data. Do not rely on controls remembered from an earlier product version.

Understand foundational measures. **Impressions** count recorded displays under the platform's definition; **reach** commonly concerns distinct accounts or people measured under platform rules; **clicks** are registered interactions with a link or ad element as defined; **CTR** is clicks divided by impressions; **CPC** is spend divided by counted clicks. **Cost per acquisition** uses an explicitly defined acquired action. These metrics are not interchangeable, and platforms may count or attribute them differently.

Budget a limited test before scaling. A comparison between two messages is useful only when major differences are controlled, data is reasonably comparable and the conversion event is reliable. A cheaper click may bring unqualified traffic. Monitor frequency, costs, landing-page failures, privacy restrictions and follow-up capacity. Pause an ad when essential claims prove false or when its destination cannot fulfill the promise.

**Calculation:** An ad spends $120 and receives 240 defined clicks. CPC = $120 / 240 = $0.50 per click. The campaign has not necessarily generated 240 customers.

**Practice:** Create a two-creative test with objective, budget ceiling, metric definitions and a decision rule for continuing or stopping.

## 08 — Influencers, Partnerships and Disclosure

Creator partnerships can introduce a service to an audience that trusts a particular communicator. The partnership should align with the audience's actual needs and the creator's authentic experience. Define deliverables, evidence for claims, usage rights, review responsibilities, timing, compensation and a procedure for handling mistakes. Do not choose partners solely by follower count: audience overlap, actual engagement quality, reputation, relevant expertise and measurable outcomes matter.

Paid posts, gifted products and other material relationships can trigger disclosure duties. U.S. Federal Trade Commission guidance describes making material relationships clear and hard to miss; other jurisdictions may impose additional or different rules. A disclosure should accompany the endorsement in an appropriate language and format, not be hidden in a long list of hashtags. Platform labeling tools can assist, but should not be assumed sufficient for all compliance needs. Always review the rules that apply to the creator, audience and campaign.

No creator should describe a product experience they did not have or make unsupported scientific, safety or business claims. Written contracts cannot turn a false testimonial into true evidence. Check reuse permissions before placing a creator's footage in an advertisement, website or additional language version. A sponsored partnership and an independent editorial review are not the same kind of content and should not be confused.

**Fictional example:** A creator receives a complimentary software subscription and discusses it publicly. The benefit is material to the relationship and should be appropriately disclosed where required, even if there was no cash payment.

**Practice:** Draft a creator brief with explicit sponsorship identification, deliverables, claim-checking steps, usage limitations and correction procedure.

## 09 — Tracking, Funnels, Attribution and Decision Metrics

Marketing measurement connects communication to observable actions. A reporting plan should define source, date range, counting unit and exclusions for each metric. A campaign dashboard may display impressions, clicks, site visits, qualified inquiries and completed purchases, but each can be measured by different systems. Tracking tags and analytics events can be useful when implemented lawfully and accurately; respect consent and applicable privacy requirements.

A funnel helps locate where an audience leaves the journey, but should not automatically be treated as a fixed path every person follows. For example, a person may read a technical article, later return directly and then send an inquiry. Attribution models assign credit in different ways, so a channel's credited conversions may differ from the business's unique customer records. Google Analytics distinguishes key events from advertising conversions and documents settings that can change reported credit; do not assume the two labels always produce identical counts.

Use arithmetic carefully. Conversion rate should name its denominator, such as 30 completed forms out of 600 eligible landing-page visits = 5%. ROAS equals revenue attributed to ads divided by advertising spend under stated rules, but is **not** profit or return on total business investment. Costs of fulfillment, staff and refunds still matter. Check whether high performance is a real improvement or a change in reporting definitions.

**Fictional example:** A campaign reports 40 form completions, yet the sales team identifies 22 qualified inquiries. The dashboard can be correct on its own definition while still overstating the leads useful to sales.

**Practice:** Build a simple dashboard with five measures, denominator definitions and an explanation of how attribution might change the reported result.

## 10 — Experiments, Ethics, Privacy and Strategy Adjustment

Marketing tests should answer a specific decision question. Define the initial hypothesis, intervention, comparison condition, eligible audience and primary outcome before publishing competing creatives. Change one important element where practical, such as the clarity of a call to action, rather than simultaneously changing format, audience and offer. If the campaign produces very few reliable conversions, apparent percentage differences may be unstable. Do not scale solely because one post performed well for a day.

Review balancing outcomes: complaints, unqualified inquiries, delivery capacity, cost per useful lead, refunds and potential exclusion of important customer groups. A campaign that boosts clicks through misleading promises is not a successful strategy. Discuss results as observed evidence, not guaranteed cause. Where feasible, use matched or randomized experiments and document concurrent changes such as seasonality or website revisions.

Privacy and responsible targeting should be built into planning. Avoid collecting unnecessary sensitive identifiers or importing customer lists without proper rights and legal basis. Maintain access controls for advertising accounts, analytics and community inboxes. Marketing teams must not fabricate user testimonials or manipulate images to imply false results. Record who approved final creative and the process for removing an inaccurate live advertisement.

**Fictional example:** Two adverts receive similar clicks, but the cheaper advert produces mostly unsuitable inquiries. The decision should consider qualified outcomes and the cost of handling irrelevant leads rather than clicks alone.

**Practice:** Draft a test plan with one hypothesis, one primary metric, two balancing measures, a stop condition and a responsible reviewer.

## 11 — Worked Campaign: Website Design Services

**Entirely fictional campaign.** A small design agency wants inquiries from restaurants needing modern websites. Its existing sales team can handle 20 qualified inquiries in a month. The campaign objective is to attract at least 15 suitable inquiries in 30 days, using no more than $300 in paid media. An awareness post explains mobile menus; a consideration post demonstrates a hypothetical reservation flow; and a conversion post links to a real service inquiry form. The hypothetical demonstration must not claim to be an existing customer's documented outcome.

Build two audience segments: owners starting a new restaurant and managers updating an established site. Identify different objections, such as setup price versus migration downtime. Prepare two creative versions that differ in a meaningful variable. Check that the website link works and the inquiry form records consent information appropriately. Schedule posts, designate a responder and write a customer service escalation path.

**Worked data:** Creative A spends $120, produces 240 clicks and 12 form completions. CPC = $0.50 and form-completion rate per click = 5%. Creative B spends $120, produces 160 clicks and 16 form completions. CPC = $0.75 and completion rate per click = 10%. If A generates 4 qualified inquiries and B generates 10, B provides more useful inquiries at $12 per qualified inquiry compared with A's $30, under these fictional observations. Sample size, attribution and actual service results still need examination.

**Application:** Prepare a 30-day calendar, two creative briefs, a rights/disclosure check, an outcome dashboard and a one-page recommendation. Explain whether to adjust the objective if the team's capacity is reached. Include a correction plan if one ad's service promise proves inaccurate.

## 12 — Self-Test, Strategy Project and Reference Notes

**Self-test:** (1) How does an objective differ from a posting target? (2) Why should personas be treated as hypotheses? (3) What makes a content pillar useful? (4) Why must a content calendar include owners and approvals? (5) What is CPC? (6) Why do clicks not equal customers? (7) What relationship may require creator disclosure? (8) Why do key events and ad conversions sometimes differ? (9) What is the difference between ROAS and profit? (10) Which balancing outcomes might change a decision to scale?

**Final assignment:** Design a 30-day original campaign for a fictional business. Submit a measurable objective and audience segmentation; two channel choices with rationales; three content pillars; an eight-item calendar with rights and approval fields; one short video script; a landing-page call to action; a test budget and performance formula sheet; and a report showing one cautious improvement decision. Calculate at least CPC, click-to-form rate and cost per qualified inquiry from supplied hypothetical numbers. Mark fabricated examples as such.

**Practice evaluation rubric:** Clear objective and customer value 20%; research and channel fit 15%; creative quality and accurate claims 20%; ethical disclosure, privacy and permissions 15%; measurement and arithmetic 20%; feasibility and learning plan 10%. The exercise is for practice and does not confer an MAIC credential or any third-party marketing certification.
