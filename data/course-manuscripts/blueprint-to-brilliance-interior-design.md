## 02 — Interior Design as Function, Experience and Space

Interior design creates useful and coherent environments for human activities. It combines spatial organization, ergonomics, lighting, materials, visual identity, safety, access and budget. Attractive finishes cannot compensate for a blocked exit, inadequate circulation or a workspace that cannot accommodate its intended tasks. A design proposal should respond to real occupants and constraints rather than a mood-board trend alone.

Learners should be able to gather a project brief, document existing conditions, translate activities into room requirements, prepare scaled layout sketches, assess circulation, develop lighting and color concepts, select materials responsibly, create a furniture plan, estimate a simple budget, communicate a design and test it against functional and safety criteria. The goal is a readable concept package with clear boundaries and evidence.

**Eight-hour guided plan:** Orientation 20 minutes; sections on pages 3–10 at 45 minutes each (360 minutes); worked room concept on page 11 for 60 minutes; final review on page 12 for 40 minutes. Total 480 minutes. Sketches and basic digital drawing are sufficient for the study project; specialized software can be used where available.

**Starter exercise:** Sketch a small reading room. Identify entry, seating, desk activity, storage, lighting and a clear route through the space. Explain one choice based on use rather than decoration.

## 03 — Client Brief, Users and Existing Conditions

Begin with an evidence-based project brief. Interview the client about the intended activities, number and needs of occupants, operating times, maintenance capacity, budget and style preferences. Distinguish a request such as “make it modern” from requirements such as seating six people, storing books or providing glare-free computer workstations. The two may coexist but have different verification methods.

Measure the space carefully, including wall lengths, openings, window positions, ceiling heights, fixed equipment and services. Draw an existing-condition plan with units, known obstructions and uncertainty notes. A photograph or real-estate listing is not a substitute for site measurement. Architectural or structural changes require appropriate professional and regulatory review.

Map users and stakeholders. A training room may need flexible layouts and good acoustics; a reading room needs quiet and accessible circulation. Interview findings are not population statistics, and personal preferences should not be described as universal ergonomic principles. Record fire safety, accessibility and building systems as requirements to check with local qualified professionals.

**Fictional example:** A client's target is six workstations, but the measured room has two awkward door swings. The design must test circulation before committing to six desks.

**Practice:** Draft a one-page brief, an existing-condition checklist and five neutral questions about occupants and use.

## 04 — Scaled Plans, Zoning and Circulation

A floor plan translates the room into measurable relationships. Use a scale and consistent units so furniture and circulation can be compared accurately. Zoning groups compatible activities: focused work, storage, waiting, discussion or circulation. Do not divide the room simply into visually balanced rectangles if the actual workflow depends on a different arrangement.

Circulation paths connect entrances, furnishings and exit routes. Widths and clearances should follow the relevant local codes and accessibility rules for the building and use; no single classroom measurement is universally sufficient. Sketch door swings, furniture footprints and likely movement. Avoid placing bookcases where they restrict egress or create collisions.

Explore alternatives before choosing one. A plan with furniture along two walls may preserve an open middle area, while a central table may better support group activity. Compare how users move, where daylight falls, and whether equipment can be reached. A design should document its assumptions and constraints instead of claiming code compliance based on appearance.

**Worked example:** A fictional room is 5 m by 4 m, giving 20 m² of nominal floor area. A fixed 2 m² storage zone leaves 18 m² before accounting for desks and paths; that does not automatically establish safe capacity for any number of people.

**Practice:** Produce two scaled furniture layouts and mark the primary movement path and potential obstructions.

## 05 — Human Factors, Furniture and Flexibility

Ergonomics studies how products and environments fit the activities and characteristics of users. Furniture planning should consider reach, posture, adjustability, visual comfort and ease of movement. One chair height is not suitable for every person. Rather than relying only on standard-looking pictures, choose furnishings that can be tested or adjusted for the intended occupants.

Flexible spaces may support different activities through movable furniture, modular storage or adaptable lighting. But movable objects still require stable construction, safe electrical management and clear storage when not in use. Accessibility involves reach, turning, doorway and route issues that depend on the actual project and jurisdiction. Designers should coordinate technical dimensions with appropriate guidance and professional assessment.

Develop a furniture schedule with item, dimensions, quantity, intended use, finish and maintenance. A visually identical replacement may have different dimensions or performance; confirm product data before ordering. A design plan should distinguish confirmed product specifications from placeholders.

**Fictional example:** A six-person study table needs room for chairs to be used and accessed. The plan must account for occupied chair positions and adjacent circulation, not only the tabletop footprint.

**Practice:** Make a furniture inventory and annotate one layout showing how different users reach storage and controls.

## 06 — Lighting, Daylight and Visual Comfort

Lighting affects safety, mood, perception and task performance. Daylight can support a welcoming atmosphere but may also create glare or heat gains. Artificial lighting should be layered according to function: ambient illumination for general movement, task lighting for focused work and accent lighting for selected visual emphasis. An attractive chandelier is not a substitute for adequate desk illumination.

Assess windows, sun direction, reflective surfaces and expected activities. A screen placed directly opposite a bright window may produce uncomfortable reflections; blinds or workstation orientation may improve visual comfort. Use manufacturer photometric data or qualified lighting design for real performance requirements. Do not describe a fictional lighting sketch as measured lux performance.

Color temperatures and color rendering can affect perceived materials and task visibility, while energy consumption and maintenance influence long-term costs. Plan controls appropriate to occupants and consider daylight-responsive settings where feasible. Electrical modifications must follow applicable codes and be completed by qualified people.

**Fictional example:** A reading area near a large window has good natural light at noon but difficult glare on laptop screens. The designer proposes adjustable shading and a task-lighting option, subject to site testing.

**Practice:** Draw a lighting zoning sketch and list four questions to verify before purchasing fixtures.

## 07 — Color, Materials, Acoustics and Maintenance

An interior palette can establish identity and support visual clarity. Choose colors for their actual surface area and lighting context rather than relying on small screen swatches. Dark finishes may create a calm effect but can reduce apparent brightness; reflective materials can amplify glare. Contrast is especially important where people must distinguish steps, handles, doors or signs.

Materials have technical and operational properties. Flooring selection involves wear, slip risk, cleaning, moisture and acoustic performance. Upholstery needs appropriate maintenance and durability. Surface emissions, fire characteristics and product suitability may be governed by local standards and manufacturer data. An online photograph cannot establish that a material is appropriate for a public building.

Acoustic comfort matters in reading and collaborative spaces. Hard reflective surfaces may increase reverberation; absorptive treatments may help but should be chosen based on the actual room and fire-safety needs. Do not claim a material “soundproofs” a space without measured performance. Use sample boards and site mockups to assess appearance and practical cleaning.

**Fictional example:** The design proposes a washable low-glare floor and fabric acoustic panels. The proposal identifies product-specific safety and maintenance data that still require verification.

**Practice:** Prepare a sample palette with three surface roles, two flooring alternatives and a maintenance checklist.

## 08 — Accessibility, Safety and Inclusive Interior Design

Inclusive design considers people with varied mobility, vision, hearing and cognitive needs. A route may seem wide in an empty rendering but become blocked by opened drawers or occupied chairs. Doors, turning spaces, signs, lighting and furniture placement must be assessed in real use. The applicable accessibility framework depends on the building and location; do not substitute a generic internet diagram for binding local requirements.

Life safety has priority. Avoid obstructing exits or placing flammable materials near unsafe equipment. Fire detection, protection, occupancy limits and electrical systems require competent design and approvals. A classroom floor plan cannot establish compliance with the fire code, accessible route rules or structural requirements. Record these as professional verification steps before implementation.

Wayfinding should be understandable without relying solely on a color cue. Clear labels, stable routes, sufficient contrast, intuitive placement and appropriate lighting help users find destinations. Users may also need acoustic privacy, adjustable workstations or alternatives to complex controls. Seek direct feedback from relevant people and avoid assuming one design works identically for everyone.

**Fictional example:** A storage cabinet door swings into the primary walking path. The revised plan relocates it, and the team records a site safety check to be conducted before installation.

**Practice:** Annotate an example plan with six inclusive-design checks, two unresolved code questions and an emergency-egress concern.

## 09 — Concept Boards, Visualizations and Documentation

An interior concept board communicates materials, colors, form, lighting and atmosphere. It is not itself a buildable plan. Pair inspirational imagery with a dimensioned layout and factual design notes so the reviewer can distinguish creative direction from engineering detail. Use original or rights-cleared reference images, and avoid copying a competitor's protected visual package or inserting unrelated school branding.

Visualizations can help nontechnical clients understand the proposal. A perspective image may show furniture proportions and mood, but it can hide aisle widths or ceiling conflicts. Include a floor plan, furniture schedule and at least one section or elevation where necessary. Label drawings by scale, date and revision. If a rendering depicts a fictional room, do not present it as a photograph of a completed installation.

Communicate alternatives and decisions. A client should see why one concept meets activities and constraints better than another. A material sample may change appearance under real lighting; provide a physical mockup or vendor sample before final selection where feasible. Keep approved identity assets consistent across boards.

**Fictional example:** Two renderings show the same reading room in warm and cool palettes. The comparative plan and furniture dimensions remain identical, so viewers can assess the color direction without confusing the spatial proposal.

**Practice:** Create a concept board, dimensioned plan and three revision notes for an original fictional room.

## 10 — Budget, Scheduling and Coordination

A useful interior design budget covers more than furniture purchase. Consider finishes, installation, lighting, professional services, permits where applicable, delivery, tax, disposal, contingency and maintenance. Use quotes tied to defined quantities, dates and locations. A beautifully low budget based on missing items is not a reliable cost plan.

Schedule work by dependencies. Measurements precede final furniture orders; approved technical plans precede construction changes; materials should be ordered with realistic lead times. Site access, safety, noise and operational interruptions need coordination. A change in one dimension may affect several trades. Designers should maintain an issue log so clients can see what remains unresolved.

Calculate examples transparently. If six fictional chairs cost $120 each, the chair subtotal is $720 before applicable taxes or delivery. If the remaining furniture budget is $1,000, only $280 remains for other furniture, not for all unpriced construction work. Cost estimates are not vendor quotations and should be marked as examples.

**Fictional example:** The client chooses a different desk depth after electrical outlets were planned. The team revisits circulation and power locations before ordering, rather than assuming the new desk fits.

**Practice:** Prepare a seven-item budget, simple schedule and dependency diagram with one contingency.

## 11 — Worked Case: A Community Reading Room

**Entirely fictional studio case.** A library has a rectangular room measuring 5 m × 4 m with an existing door and window. The client wants a reading zone for six people, modest book storage, a small information desk, comfortable lighting and accessible movement. The exact door opening and local building code requirements are not specified; they must be verified before any real implementation.

Prepare a measured-room checklist and basic 20 m² floor plan. Make two alternative layouts: one central shared table and one with desks along the walls. Mark how users move from the entrance to seating, storage and the exit. Add furniture footprints at scale. Compare the alternatives for workflow, potential glare, noise, visibility and maintenance. Do not assert either layout satisfies an unspecified code.

**Example arithmetic:** Six chairs at a hypothetical $120 each cost $720. Two lamps at $85 each cost $170, giving $890 before delivery, taxes, installation or other items. If the room has a $2,500 overall fictional project allowance, $1,610 remains for other specified costs; this remaining figure is not a complete construction budget.

Create a palette with a neutral background, one accent color and readable wayfinding treatment. Show ambient, task and optional accent lighting roles. Write three safety/accessibility checks requiring site verification. Present a concept board and one accurate perspective or annotated sketch, stating it is illustrative rather than a completed room.

**Deliverables:** Brief, existing-condition questions, two scaled alternatives, material and lighting notes, furniture schedule, budget calculations, review checklist and a preferred-option rationale.

## 12 — Review, Portfolio and Further References

**Self-test:** (1) What does an interior design brief contain? (2) Why must a site be measured? (3) What is zoning? (4) Why must circulation include occupied furniture? (5) How can flexible design improve a room? (6) What is the difference between ambient and task lighting? (7) Why is a mood board insufficient as a technical deliverable? (8) What safety and accessibility work needs qualified review? (9) Why should estimates include installation and contingency? (10) What can a client infer from a visualization versus a measured drawing?

**Final portfolio:** Produce an original fictional reading-room proposal: a 200-word client brief; site survey checklist; two dimensioned concept layouts; furniture and material schedule; lighting/visual-comfort proposal; a short accessibility and egress review list; a budget with assumptions; and one visualization or annotated perspective. Compare the concepts against activities, maintenance, budget and unresolved safety requirements.

**Practice rubric:** Brief and measured-space reasoning 20%; zoning and circulation 20%; furniture and human factors 15%; lighting and materials 15%; accessible, safe planning 15%; budget and communication 15%. Classroom evaluation is not official professional licensure, building approval or academic certification.

**Reference guidance:** Use the applicable local building and accessibility codes, fire authority and product manufacturers for any real project. W3C principles on readable information (https://www.w3.org/WAI/) may inform digital presentation of the concept, but cannot replace physical building accessibility standards.
