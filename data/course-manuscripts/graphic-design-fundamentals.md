## 02 — Design as Visual Problem-Solving

Graphic design is the deliberate arrangement of text, images, color and space to communicate a message to a defined audience. The job is not merely to make a surface attractive: a successful design helps viewers find the correct information, understand the next action and recognize the intended identity. A polished visual can still fail if its message is unclear, inaccurate or unreadable. Designers should begin with purpose and evidence before selecting fonts or decorative effects.

By the end of this introductory course, learners should be able to write a design brief, establish a visual hierarchy, use alignment and spacing, compare typographic options, explain basic color relationships, distinguish raster and vector assets, adapt a layout to different formats, check contrast and legibility, prepare a simple print or screen export and critique a design against a practical objective.

**Guided six-hour study route:** Orientation 20 minutes; eight technique sections on pages 3–10 at 35 minutes each (280 minutes); worked project on page 11 for 45 minutes; and final design check on page 12 for 15 minutes. Total: 360 minutes. Use an accessible design application or paper sketches; the methods are software-independent. More complex projects will naturally take additional time beyond this introductory route.

**Starter exercise:** Imagine a small community workshop announcing its opening date and contact details. Make two rough sketches: one dominated by decoration, the other prioritizing date, place and action. Explain which better serves people who have only a few seconds to read it.

## 03 — The Brief, Audience and Meaning

Every design needs a purpose, target audience and delivery context. A poster seen from several meters away is not read like a detailed web page viewed on a phone. A product label must work at packaging scale; a public information notice may need multiple languages and prominent actionable details. Before opening design software, collect the exact message, approved identity rules, available imagery, required sizes, publication deadline and contact or legal text that must remain correct.

A practical design brief includes the problem, audience, goal, mandatory information, visual tone, channel, dimensions, accessibility concerns, rights constraints and review owner. State a success measure connected to comprehension. For a workshop poster, success might be a reader accurately identifying the start date, location and registration route after a brief glance. Social likes alone would not prove the poster communicates correctly.

Separate confirmed material from unverified claims. A client's proposed guarantee is not automatically factual evidence. Ask for the appropriate support before typesetting the statement as a bold promotional headline. Request original logo artwork and permissions for photographs. If approved assets are unavailable, create a clearly labeled placeholder for review rather than copying a competitor's brand template.

**Fictional example:** A library requests a poster for a reading event. The brief includes Saturday 10 a.m., a defined address, an attendance limit and a registration link. The poster must not invent free admission if the fee has not been confirmed.

**Practice:** Write a one-paragraph brief and list five non-negotiable items before drawing three thumbnail layout options.

## 04 — Visual Hierarchy, Grids and Alignment

Hierarchy determines which element attracts attention first, second and third. Size, placement, contrast, weight and spacing can establish that order. The largest element should usually communicate the principal message rather than serve as arbitrary decoration. Group related information through proximity and consistent formatting. If the date, time and location are separated by unrelated illustrations, a reader may miss the sequence of details needed to attend.

Alignment gives elements a visible relationship. A simple grid defines repeatable columns, margins and spacing, reducing accidental jumps between cards, paragraphs or headings. Grids are guides, not decorative cages. Choose an arrangement that supports the message and adapt it to each format. Random alignment can produce visual noise even when individual components are well drawn.

White space is the unused area that helps separate groups and control visual density. Do not fill every gap simply to appear productive. A clear margin can increase the perceived connection between a heading and its supporting text, or distinguish a call to action from background material. Visual balance can be symmetrical or asymmetrical, but the result should feel deliberate and easy to scan.

**Fictional example:** A two-column event announcement places the event name above, key date and venue in the first column, and a concise description plus QR code in the second. Margins remain consistent even when localized text becomes longer.

**Practice:** Rebuild a cluttered fictional flyer using three information levels and one grid. Explain the order in which a first-time reader will notice its elements.

## 05 — Typography, Reading and Language

Typography communicates both meaning and character. Select fonts that support the brand and the reading context. A display typeface can provide personality in a short heading but may be tiring in longer body paragraphs. Set a clear hierarchy using size, weight, line spacing and consistent spacing rather than relying on an excessive number of unrelated fonts. Decorative lettering should not make necessary instructions hard to read.

Measure legibility at the size and medium at which people will see the design. Thin text over a detailed photograph may disappear on mobile screens. Long centered paragraphs can be harder to follow than short, consistently aligned lines. Avoid stretched or distorted text, inconsistent punctuation, and poor line breaks that separate related words. Check number formatting, currencies, names, dates and links carefully because these are real information, not optional decoration.

Multilingual design requires planning. Arabic uses a different script and writing direction from English; a logo wordmark may remain an unchanged identity asset while body text is translated. Content expansion, diacritics and font support can affect line breaks. Treat each language version as a separate tested layout rather than assuming automatic word replacement preserves visual balance.

**Fictional example:** An event's name is in a bold display face, while admission conditions and address use a simpler legible font. The supporting type remains readable without zooming excessively.

**Practice:** Build a type scale with headline, subheading, body and caption. Test each on a mobile-sized frame and a printed A4 mockup.

## 06 — Color, Contrast and Meaning

Color can create association, emotion, emphasis and consistent identity, but it should not be the only way a viewer understands a critical message. Choose a limited palette based on the design brief and apply it systematically. A primary brand color may highlight headings or actions, while neutral tones support information-heavy areas. Always check the contrast between text and background; appealing combinations can still fail for readability.

For web content, WCAG 2.2 Level AA Success Criterion 1.4.3 generally sets minimum contrast ratios of 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for defined large text, subject to its listed exceptions. These figures are web accessibility guidance and should not be misrepresented as a universal print-production regulation. Contrast depends on relative luminance, not merely on whether two hues have different names. Use appropriate tools to evaluate real foreground and background combinations.

Meaningful colors need redundant labels or symbols where possible. An error shown only in red and a success shown only in green can be inaccessible to some viewers. The same principle applies to charts: identify categories through names, patterns or other clear visual distinctions. Consistency matters: if navy is used for primary headings throughout an academy's identity, changing it arbitrarily on one page may weaken recognition.

**Fictional example:** A blue button with barely visible pale text might fit a palette but fail readability. Increasing the contrast or adjusting the surrounding design can preserve identity while improving accessibility.

**Practice:** Choose three color roles and evaluate two text/background pairs for readability using an appropriate contrast checker.

## 07 — Images, Vector Graphics and Resolution

Raster images are made from pixels. Photographs are commonly raster assets, and enlarging a small image too far may expose visible softness or pixelation. Vector artwork describes paths and shapes that can scale without the same pixel-resolution limitation, making it useful for logos and line illustrations. A vector file may still contain embedded raster pictures, so file extension alone does not prove every element is fully scalable.

Choose image content that supports the message and for which appropriate rights exist. An attractive photograph of an unrelated building can mislead if it appears to show the actual location of an event. Stock imagery, generated art and original documentary photos have different provenance; label illustrative imagery when that distinction matters. Avoid copying protected logos or layouts from unrelated businesses simply because they are visible online.

Technical output requirements depend on use. Screen graphics are often specified in pixel dimensions and aspect ratios; print workflows may need actual physical dimensions, suitable effective image resolution, bleed and color specifications. A pixel count cannot by itself determine the final print quality without considering the intended physical size and viewing distance. Test the actual output rather than relying on one universal DPI rule.

**Fictional example:** A community logo built as editable vector shapes remains sharp on stationery and a large banner, while a 150-pixel screenshot of the logo may become blurry when enlarged.

**Practice:** Compare an original photo, a compressed thumbnail and a scalable vector mark. Document when each is suitable and why.

## 08 — Branding, Consistency and Template Discipline

Visual identity includes approved logo artwork, typography, color roles, spacing, image treatment and tone. Consistency helps an audience recognize the same organization across a website, certificate, social post and printed brochure. It does not mean every item must use an identical layout. A mobile story and a wide desktop banner require different composition while retaining the same recognizable identity principles.

Create a small set of reusable components with documented dimensions and safe margins: header, title block, body copy, call-to-action, contact block and image frame. Lock identity elements that should never change through translation or resizing, and test alternative language versions separately. Do not allow automatic layout adjustments to move key navigation or branding unpredictably. Review both desktop and mobile exports after edits.

Templates reduce repetitive work but can create careless duplication. Each new design still needs verified date, address, fee, program title and contact information. Avoid copied watermarks, external academy promotions or third-party course branding. Keep a master template with change history, then save a new file for each approved release. An edit request should not silently replace a previously approved logo.

**Fictional example:** A design system uses navy, antique gold and a consistent crest for academy materials, but poster and certificate layouts have different information hierarchies. Both can feel related without being identical.

**Practice:** Define six fixed identity rules, four editable fields and one review procedure for a reusable event-poster template.

## 09 — Digital Layout, Responsiveness and Accessibility

Digital graphics can appear on small mobile screens, tablets, desktop monitors and projections. A design that reads well at full desktop size can become illegible when reduced for social media. Plan hierarchy around the smallest important context of use. Keep essential information inside safe visual boundaries and avoid relying on small text embedded in complex imagery. Test actual crops and overlays on target devices.

Responsive design may require rearranging groups rather than simply scaling them down. For example, two balanced columns on desktop may become a single vertical sequence on a narrow screen. The reading order must remain sensible and complete; key text should not disappear behind a fixed interface element. If a design contains interactive content, ensure labels and contrast support its actual function rather than merely looking like clickable decoration.

Accessibility reaches beyond contrast. Provide useful alternative text where appropriate, avoid meaning conveyed through color alone, preserve actual readable text instead of unnecessary text-as-image, and do not let animation or visual clutter obscure critical instructions. A social graphic may have less room for detail, so the caption and linked accessible page should provide the information that cannot be legibly displayed.

**Fictional example:** A landscape workshop banner includes title, date and registration. Its mobile version stacks those items vertically without rotating the logo or pushing the registration link offscreen.

**Practice:** Sketch desktop and phone versions of the same message. Verify a consistent identity, clear reading order and unchanged factual details.

## 10 — Print Preparation, File Formats and Quality Control

Print design introduces physical production constraints. Confirm trim size, safe margins, bleed, paper and printer specifications before exporting. Artwork intended to reach the paper edge may require bleed beyond the trim line; critical text should stay inside safe margins. Exact settings vary by print provider and product. Discuss color handling and profile requirements with the printer instead of assuming that a screen preview will exactly reproduce ink on paper.

Select file formats for the task. An editable design source is useful for future revisions; a PDF may be appropriate for review or print handoff; a PNG may suit graphics that need transparency; JPEG can be efficient for photographs but may be inappropriate for assets that require transparent backgrounds. Check font embedding and image quality when delivering PDFs, and inspect the result outside the design application. A correct-looking preview cannot guarantee that the exported file is suitable.

Quality assurance is editorial as well as technical. Verify names, phone numbers, prices, dates, URLs, spelling, logo proportions and image rights. Check cropping, alignment, line breaks and color use at final size. Record the file version and approvals. A correction to an incorrect event date should create a traceable updated export rather than quietly leaving outdated copies in circulation.

**Fictional example:** A printed poster contains a gold frame that must reach the edges. The designer asks the printer for bleed requirements and keeps the contact number safely within the trim area.

**Practice:** Build separate checklists for a screen PNG and a print PDF; explain why the technical checks differ.

## 11 — Worked Case: A Workshop Launch Poster

**Entirely fictional brief.** A community design workshop will run a free introductory session on Saturday, December 5, 2026, from 10 a.m. to noon at a clearly specified venue. The project needs an A4 print poster and a 1080-by-1350-pixel social graphic. The event is free only in this fictional exercise. The organizer supplies approved logo artwork, official contact text and an original photograph; no third-party template or competitor branding is permitted.

Create a hierarchy of event title, date/time, location, short benefit description and registration action. Use one consistent identity palette and no more type styles than needed. Choose a simple grid and safe margins. For the social version, adapt the layout to the vertical aspect ratio rather than stretching the print design. Keep the logo upright, leave room for app overlays, and test the contact information on a phone-sized preview.

For print, confirm actual trim and bleed requirements rather than inventing universal values. Check whether the photograph has sufficient effective resolution at the chosen printed size. For digital, inspect text contrast, legible sizing and image context. If the workshop offers only 30 spaces, state that limit only if confirmed by the fictional source brief. A QR code should resolve to a real applicable destination in a real campaign; use a clearly marked placeholder in this classroom case.

**Application:** Produce two layout sketches, a source/rights checklist, a simple type hierarchy and separate final-export QA notes. Explain how the design changes if the event title is translated into a longer language. Write a correction plan for a wrong venue address discovered after publication.

## 12 — Knowledge Check, Portfolio Exercise and References

**Self-test:** (1) What information belongs in a design brief? (2) What is visual hierarchy? (3) How does alignment improve a layout? (4) Why do long paragraphs need legible body type? (5) What is the purpose of a contrast ratio? (6) How do vector and raster assets differ? (7) Why should a brand template contain fixed and editable elements? (8) Why is a desktop graphic not always a suitable mobile graphic? (9) What is the purpose of print bleed? (10) What should be checked after exporting a design?

**Portfolio task:** Develop a coordinated print and digital pair for the fictional workshop case or another clearly labeled original scenario. Submit a one-page brief, three thumbnail concepts, selected palette and typography scale, editable layout or annotated sketch, A4 and social compositions, rights and accessibility notes, export settings, and a short critique. Compare the final work against reader comprehension, identity consistency and reliable contact details.

**Suggested learning rubric:** Audience and message 20%; hierarchy and alignment 20%; typography and color readability 20%; appropriate images and rights 10%; responsive and print adaptation 15%; factual and technical QA 15%. This rubric is for study feedback, not an official MAIC exam, external design certification or degree claim.

**Further reading:** W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2, including Understanding Success Criterion 1.4.3 on text contrast, https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html . The criterion applies to specified web content, with stated exceptions, and is not a substitute for a full print preflight. Follow current requirements of the actual printer or digital platform.
