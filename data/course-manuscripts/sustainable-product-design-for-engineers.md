## 02 — Engineers as Designers of Product Systems

Sustainable product design seeks to meet useful human needs while reducing avoidable environmental and social impacts across a product's life. Engineering decisions about materials, geometry, energy, manufacturing, service life, repair and recovery influence impacts that users cannot see. A product that uses fewer grams of material may still perform worse if it fails sooner or creates more waste during use. Sustainable design should not replace performance, safety or accessibility with unsupported environmental promises.

Learners will define a functional unit and design brief, identify life-cycle stages, compare material and manufacturing options, plan for durability and repairability, recognize tradeoffs in energy and transport, explain screening life-cycle assessment, explore circular business models, assess environmental claims and create a small evidence-based redesign proposal. The approach integrates familiar engineering constraints with environmental reasoning rather than ranking materials by appearance.

**Eight-hour guided route:** Introduction 20 minutes; pages 3–10 at 45 minutes each (360 minutes); applied design case on page 11 for 60 minutes; and assessment plus reflection on page 12 for 40 minutes. Total 480 minutes. Learners may use sketches and basic spreadsheets; specialized LCA software and laboratory facilities are not required for the introductory exercises.

**Starter:** Imagine a portable desk lamp. List its primary function, expected service environment, typical failure modes, replaceable parts and the data needed before claiming any redesign uses fewer resources overall.

## 03 — Functional Requirements, Boundaries and Stakeholders

A credible redesign begins with functional equivalence. Define what the product must do, for how long, under which safety conditions and with what acceptable performance. Users may want easy handling and affordability; technicians need access to replaceable components; manufacturers need feasible assembly and quality control; recovery partners need identifiable materials. Engineering must resolve tradeoffs rather than satisfy each preference with an untested promise.

State a functional unit for comparing alternatives, such as “provide 1,000 hours of adequate desk illumination.” An estimate comparing one lamp with another is misleading if their lifetime or output differs materially. Identify the life-cycle boundary, from sourcing and manufacturing through distribution, use, maintenance and end-of-life. The functional unit and boundary help determine which quantities belong in a comparison.

Record assumptions and uncertainty. A supplier's unspecified “eco” description is not a measurement. Ask for verified material composition, durability data, repair parts, applicable electrical safety requirements and likely disposal routes. The goal is a testable engineering brief, not a single green label.

**Fictional case:** Lamp A has an estimated 1,000-hour life. Lamp B has a longer-life claim but no reliability test. The comparison cannot treat the marketing claim as a measured lifetime without appropriate evidence.

**Practice:** Write eight functional requirements and a source table distinguishing observed values from unverified claims.

## 04 — Life-Cycle Assessment and Inventory Reasoning

Life-cycle assessment (LCA) provides a structured approach to evaluating environmental aspects and impacts. ISO 14040 describes the framework: goal and scope definition, life-cycle inventory, impact assessment and interpretation, with reporting and review considerations. ISO 14040 is a framework, not a single universal formula for scoring every product. A classroom screening analysis cannot automatically be called an ISO-conforming comparative public assertion.

A life-cycle inventory organizes relevant inputs and outputs: mass of materials, manufacturing energy, transport, use-phase electricity, replacement parts and end-of-life processes. Data quality, time horizon, geographic representativeness and allocation rules can change results. One option may reduce manufacturing emissions while increasing use-phase electricity. Report these separately rather than hiding tradeoffs behind a vague “sustainability score.”

Select impact categories appropriate to the goal, such as climate change, resource use or water impacts, and avoid assuming they can be summed without value choices or a defined method. Model uncertainties and data gaps. Screening results should guide what to investigate, while formal public comparative claims may need expert methods and critical review.

**Fictional illustration:** A lamp consumes 8 W during use. For 1,000 hours, electricity consumption is 8 W × 1,000 h = 8,000 Wh, or 8 kWh. The corresponding environmental impact requires region- and method-specific information; the energy figure alone is not a carbon footprint.

**Practice:** Build a five-stage inventory template and label each entry by units, evidence quality and boundary.

## 05 — Material Selection and Manufacturing Tradeoffs

Material choice considers mechanical strength, fatigue, temperature, corrosion, electrical safety, manufacturability, cost, supply reliability and recovery. Different materials may provide the same function at different masses and service lives. Recycled content can reduce demand for virgin material in some circumstances, but the environmental result depends on processing, material losses, contaminants and the actual system. Recyclable and recycled-content are different claims.

Manufacturing processes introduce waste and energy demands. Injection molding, machining, sheet forming and additive manufacturing have different tradeoffs for a given geometry, batch size and tolerance. An additive process may avoid a costly tool for low production volumes yet produce support waste or high process energy. Do not treat a particular process as universally sustainable.

Choose materials and processes jointly. A part that is inexpensive to form but impossible to repair may create long-term waste. Design choices should meet material and safety regulations in the target market, including electrical and chemical restrictions where applicable. Record alternatives and test against a common functional unit.

**Fictional example:** A lamp housing is redesigned from a mixed-material bonded assembly to a mechanically fastened housing. The new version may improve disassembly, but its stability, impact strength, heat performance and cost still need testing.

**Practice:** Make a comparison matrix for three housing options covering six performance criteria and five environmental considerations.

## 06 — Durability, Reliability, Maintainability and Repair

Extending useful product life can reduce replacement demand when performance is maintained, but a longer service life must be demonstrated rather than asserted. Reliability is the likelihood that a product performs as intended under defined conditions. Durability refers to resistance to wear or damage; maintainability includes the ability to restore function; repairability concerns access to parts, tools, information and feasible service processes.

Identify likely failures using a structured method, such as a basic failure mode and effects analysis. Evaluate severity, likelihood and detectability without pretending an ordinal classroom score predicts a certified failure probability. Prioritize hazards affecting user safety. Make replaceable components accessible without creating unsafe exposed electrical parts. Clear service instructions may be as important as choosing screws instead of glue.

Serviceability has tradeoffs: additional fasteners can increase assembly time, yet protect a product from becoming waste after a minor failure. Modular design can simplify part replacement but may add connections and mass. A product roadmap should record what is supported by actual tests and what still requires prototype validation.

**Fictional example:** A rechargeable lamp's battery is expected to wear out before its housing. A design allowing qualified replacement may extend service life if replacement is safe, available and economically practical.

**Practice:** List five failure modes, their consequences and a realistic preventive or repair response.

## 07 — Energy Efficiency, Use Conditions and Rebound Risks

For products that consume electricity, the use phase may be significant. Calculate consumption from verified power and time rather than guessing from appearance. An efficiency claim should specify output quality and performance. A lamp using less electricity but producing inadequate light is not functionally equivalent. The total benefit also depends on user behavior, expected lifetime and the electricity system.

Define scenarios instead of assuming one universal pattern. A desk lamp might run one hour daily in one household and eight hours daily in an office. Standby consumption may matter for always-connected products. Battery charging losses, replacements and deterioration can affect overall consumption. The engineering task is to design efficient service, not just lower a number on a label.

Efficiency gains may cause behavioral changes, sometimes called rebound effects, but the size and existence of such changes require evidence. A lower operating cost might encourage longer use; it should not automatically be assumed to erase all savings. Distinguish a measured effect from a hypothesis.

**Calculation:** A 10 W lamp used 4 hours per day for 250 days consumes 10 × 4 × 250 = 10,000 Wh, or 10 kWh. An 8 W version under identical conditions consumes 8 kWh, saving 2 kWh. These are electricity savings, not directly measured emissions or financial savings.

**Practice:** Calculate consumption in three use scenarios and describe which missing data would affect a climate-impact estimate.

## 08 — Circularity, Disassembly and Recovery

Circular engineering strategies can include material reduction, longer life, maintenance, reuse, refurbishment, remanufacturing and recovery. Each pathway depends on practical collection, economics and safe operation. An assembly is not automatically recyclable because its main material has a recycling symbol. Labels, composites, coatings, joints and local processing infrastructure all influence recovery.

Design for disassembly can use accessible fasteners, sensible material labeling and a safe sequence for separating parts. However, opening a product with energized electrical components is dangerous; user maintenance and professional service should be clearly distinguished. A component intended for reuse requires inspection standards and traceability. Reusing unsafe parts simply to avoid waste is not a valid engineering outcome.

Assess the complete system. A refill or return model requires logistics and reliable customer participation. A redesigned part may be easier to separate yet require more manufacturing material; compare tradeoffs against the defined function. A transparent recovery plan includes what happens when a user does not return the product.

**Fictional example:** A lamp's housing has a standardized screw and marked material type. Technicians can replace the battery safely, but local electronic-waste handling rules still need to be verified.

**Practice:** Sketch a disassembly sequence and identify three safety risks and two material-recovery uncertainties.

## 09 — Sustainable Product Regulation and Digital Information

Environmental product regulation is market- and category-specific. The European Union's Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 establishes a framework for future product-specific requirements. It does not mean every product in every category must immediately have the same digital product passport or meet identical numeric criteria. Product-specific delegated acts and transition periods matter.

The Digital Product Passport (DPP) framework aims to make product information more accessible and traceable, potentially covering material composition, repair, reuse and environmental data. Requirements are being phased in by product group; inclusion in an indicative work plan does not itself create a mandatory DPP for every item. Engineers should check current official delegated acts, their actual dates and the target market before describing a design as compliant.

Environmental labels and comparative claims should be specific. “Contains 25% verified recycled plastic in the housing” is a testable statement if documentation supports the defined mass basis. “Planet-friendly forever” is not a meaningful engineering measurement. Document sources, revision dates, scope and unresolved compliance matters; use qualified specialist review where needed.

**Fictional example:** A lamp manufacturer planning future EU sales creates a material-information register. That preparation is sensible, but the team does not claim an EU DPP is already legally required for this lamp without checking relevant product-specific provisions.

**Practice:** Draft a material information template and a list of compliance questions that require a current official legal source.

## 10 — Prototyping, Tests, Cost and Design Decisions

Engineering concepts need prototypes and tests appropriate to their risk. A lamp redesign may require thermal, electrical, stability, drop, endurance and serviceability checks. Some tests call for accredited laboratories and qualified professionals; a classroom sketch does not establish safety certification. State the acceptance criteria before testing. Record failures and corrective actions rather than selecting only favorable data.

A design comparison can cover functional performance, safety, expected use life, manufacturing cost, repair cost, environmental screening and recoverability. Use transparent weights and assumptions if calculating a decision score. Sensitivity analysis asks whether the preferred option changes when uncertain inputs vary. Avoid treating one precise total as a substitute for engineering judgment.

A small pilot can test assembly time and service procedures, but it cannot prove a global environmental claim. Design iterations should be versioned, with materials and measurements linked to the specific prototype. If a change introduces unacceptable electrical or thermal hazards, stop and resolve those before scaling.

**Fictional example:** Two lamp housings differ in fastener count. The easier-to-open option is also less stable in a simulated drop test. The team redesigns the joints and repeats tests, rather than declaring repairability the only criterion.

**Practice:** Write a ten-point test plan, a design-decision table and one sensitivity scenario.

## 11 — Applied Case: Redesign a Portable Desk Lamp

**All data are fictional.** Lamp A provides adequate light while consuming 10 W. Its expected service life is 1,000 hours, but its internal battery is not user-replaceable. Lamp B is a proposed design consuming 8 W with a housing that can be opened by a qualified technician. Its reliability, battery serviceability and safety require testing. Assume equal light output only for the classroom electricity comparison, not as a proven commercial fact.

Define the functional unit as 1,000 hours of adequate desk illumination. Under identical usage, Lamp A consumes 10 kWh while Lamp B consumes 8 kWh, a saving of 2 kWh or 20% of A's use-phase electricity. If electricity has a hypothetical emissions factor of 0.4 kg CO2e/kWh, the illustrative difference would be 0.8 kg CO2e for the use phase. This is not a measured full life-cycle carbon saving; the manufacturing and recovery impacts are not provided.

Design two possible housings and one safe service path for the battery. Draw material-flow maps for sourcing, assembly, use, maintenance and disposal. List missing data on material masses, electricity factors, battery lifespan, safe thermal performance and recovery infrastructure. Show why choosing B solely on the use-phase calculation would be premature.

**Portfolio outputs:** Functional brief, two sketches, failure-mode list, inventory table, energy calculations, repair procedure concept, compliance questions, six test criteria and a reasoned recommendation with uncertainties.

## 12 — Knowledge Check, Engineering Portfolio and References

**Self-test:** (1) Why compare products using a functional unit? (2) Which phases are included in the ISO 14040 framework? (3) Why can lighter material be a worse choice? (4) How do repairability and durability differ? (5) What is the electricity use of a 10 W device over 1,000 hours? (6) Why do recycling claims require local context? (7) What is the purpose of a product material information register? (8) Does a planned DPP working-plan date establish an immediate obligation for all products? (9) Why is a classroom environmental score not a certified life-cycle assessment? (10) Why should engineering tests precede public reliability claims?

**Final project:** Produce an original redesign assessment for the fictional lamp, including functional unit, evidence inventory, materials matrix, two alternative structures, repair scenario, annual and lifetime energy calculations, environmental tradeoff notes, safety test plan and a recommendation. Clearly label estimates and unresolved facts. Include a statement identifying the limits of the work and any need for qualified specialist testing.

**Practice rubric:** Functional brief and safety 20%; life-cycle reasoning 20%; materials and manufacturing 15%; energy arithmetic 15%; repair and circularity 15%; test and regulatory evidence quality 15%. This self-study rubric is not an official examination result or compliance certificate.
