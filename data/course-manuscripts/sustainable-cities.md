## 02 — Cities as Interconnected Human Systems

A sustainable city supports people's needs today while managing long-term environmental, social and economic risks. Housing, transport, public space, employment, energy, water, waste and climate resilience interact. Building a visually green district cannot compensate for unaffordable housing or unreliable essential services. Good urban planning considers access, inclusion and realistic implementation together.

Learners should identify a city's interconnected systems, interpret a few Sustainable Development Goal 11 indicators, analyze housing and mobility choices, consider urban land use, outline green infrastructure, evaluate basic-service access, assess heat and flood risks, include community participation and develop a small neighborhood improvement proposal. The emphasis is on measurable outcomes and transparent tradeoffs, not branding a city “smart” because it uses digital sensors.

**Eight-hour guided route:** Orientation 20 minutes; eight sections on pages 3–10 at 45 minutes each (360 minutes); neighborhood case on page 11 for 60 minutes; portfolio assessment on page 12 for 40 minutes. Total 480 minutes, including applied exercises. Use local evidence when working on a real city; invented numbers belong only in labeled classroom cases.

**Starter:** Sketch a route from a home to a school and a health clinic. Mark places where unsafe streets, missing services or high transport costs could prevent access.

## 03 — SDG 11, Evidence and Urban Indicators

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 11 aims to make cities and human settlements inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable. Its targets cover adequate housing and services, transport, participatory planning, protection of heritage, disaster losses, environmental impacts, public spaces and links among urban and rural areas. Not all targets have one universal indicator, and country or city comparisons require consistent definitions and source dates.

An indicator measures something specific. Public-transport access may be defined by proximity under a published methodology; a headline about the number of buses does not automatically establish equitable access. Air quality metrics have units, measurement methods and temporal context. When reporting informal settlements, definitions and available records affect estimates. Clearly separate observations from forecasts.

The UN's 2026 SDG reporting documents significant remaining challenges in housing, transit and public space, but a short training exercise should not claim a global figure describes one particular district. Choose indicators relevant to the local question, identify the official sources and disclose data limitations.

**Fictional example:** A neighborhood has 1,000 households and 240 are beyond the defined transit-access threshold. The proportion without convenient access under that simplified rule is 24%, but real measurement requires a consistent transport-access methodology.

**Practice:** Select three SDG 11 indicators and write the numerator, denominator, source and interpretation limit for each.

## 04 — Housing, Land Use and Urban Form

Housing sustainability involves affordability, safety, tenure security, service access and environmental performance. Adding buildings without schools, utilities or transport can worsen daily conditions. Urban form affects travel distances, infrastructure cost and access to employment. The appropriate density depends on context, infrastructure and local needs; low density and high density are not automatically good or bad in every situation.

Mixed-use and compact planning may reduce some travel needs by placing homes near services. However, poorly managed density can strain water, drainage and open space. Evaluate outcomes rather than a fashionable planning label. Real land-use changes require legal planning processes, community engagement and analysis of displacement risk. Historic neighborhoods may have cultural value that cannot be replaced by a rendering.

Maps should distinguish existing land use, approved plans and speculative proposals. A polygon colored green does not prove that land is publicly accessible or ecologically suitable. Consider cost, maintenance and who benefits from each design change.

**Fictional example:** A proposed apartment cluster includes 200 new homes but no new safe walking route to the clinic. The improvement plan must address everyday access, not just housing count.

**Practice:** Map an illustrative neighborhood and list two housing, two service and two displacement concerns.

## 05 — Sustainable Mobility and Street Design

Mobility connects people to opportunities. A useful transport strategy considers walking, cycling, public transport, freight, disability access, safety and affordability. Infrastructure investments should be evaluated for who can use them and the actual travel tasks they support. A bus stop near a home is not valuable if service is unreliable, unaffordable or inaccessible.

Streets are shared public environments. Design may need safe crossings, sidewalks, shade, lighting, speed management and access for emergency vehicles and deliveries. Exact standards and clearances depend on local regulations and engineering context. A streetscape image cannot establish road safety; it needs appropriate observations, risk analysis and qualified design.

Measure outcomes such as travel time, service reliability, collision risk, accessibility and emissions under defined conditions. A rise in cycle lane length does not prove cyclists can safely reach destinations if routes end abruptly. Consider connections and maintenance.

**Fictional example:** A corridor has a painted bicycle lane interrupted at a major junction. The critical issue may be continuity and crossing safety rather than adding another isolated lane segment.

**Practice:** Audit a hypothetical one-kilometer corridor for four modes of travel and identify three missing data sources.

## 06 — Water, Waste, Energy and Essential Urban Services

Cities depend on water supply, sanitation, electricity, solid-waste management and reliable communication. Service quality involves both coverage and continuity. A pipe network shown on a map may not guarantee safe water at the tap. Energy systems can have supply, cost and resilience challenges that affect residents unevenly. Urban sustainability should protect basic service functions before adding optional smart features.

Waste planning begins with prevention, collection, sorting, processing and safe disposal. Waste-generation rates and collection percentages must be measured using clear denominators. Recyclability of a product does not prove that a city can recover it. Water management includes leakage, flood drainage, wastewater treatment and watershed conditions. Green infrastructure may complement engineered systems but should be designed using realistic soils and rainfall records.

Energy planning considers demand management, building efficiency, supply choices and backup for critical services. Claims about emissions savings depend on technical assumptions and local electricity context. Prioritize access and reliability alongside resource efficiency.

**Fictional example:** A district generates 100 tonnes of waste per week, and 80 tonnes enter controlled collection. The observed collection share is 80%, but the remaining 20 tonnes and the treatment destination must be investigated.

**Practice:** Prepare a five-indicator service dashboard and identify what evidence would validate each result.

## 07 — Climate Risks, Urban Heat and Resilience

Cities face risks from heat, intense rainfall, floods, drought and other hazards depending on location. Vulnerability depends on exposure, social conditions and the ability to respond. A climate-resilience plan should use actual hazard evidence and protect people with limited resources or mobility. One tree-planting campaign alone cannot solve an unaddressed heat or flood system problem.

Assess urban heat through surface cover, shade, building form, ventilation and local weather. Shading and cooler materials may help, but measured effectiveness varies by context. Flood management may combine drainage maintenance, permeable surfaces, retention areas, land-use controls and emergency planning. Avoid presenting conceptual pond locations as engineered hydraulic capacity.

Resilience also requires governance. Early warning, public communication, critical facility continuity and maintenance responsibilities matter. A map of possible flood zones should identify its source and uncertainty, especially if used for property decisions. Qualified professionals must review safety-critical designs.

**Fictional example:** A neighborhood street floods after storms. The class can propose questions about drainage capacity and blockage, but it cannot declare a new landscape basin sufficient without site calculations.

**Practice:** Make a simple risk matrix showing hazard, exposed groups, available evidence, possible intervention and verification owner.

## 08 — Public Spaces, Health and Inclusive Participation

Public spaces can support physical activity, social connection, cooling and cultural life. Access is not just acreage: people need safe routes, usable facilities and welcoming conditions. A fenced park with restricted hours may not serve the same population as an open neighborhood square. Consider children, older people, people with disabilities and those who feel unsafe or excluded.

Participation helps reveal lived problems and build legitimacy. Public consultation should be understandable, accessible and honest about which decisions remain open. A meeting with a handful of highly engaged residents does not automatically represent all affected households. Use multiple methods and document differences in needs, rather than averaging away minority barriers.

Urban planning can have unequal costs. Improvements may raise local rents or displace existing residents; fairness requires assessing who benefits, who bears cost and what safeguards are possible. Culture and historic identity should be considered alongside new construction.

**Fictional example:** A public square is planned with benches but no accessible route or evening lighting. The project review examines who would be excluded.

**Practice:** Write a consultation plan and two success measures for an improved small public space.

## 09 — Data, Digital Tools and Responsible Smart Cities

Digital mapping, sensors and dashboards may help cities observe service gaps and plan responses, but technology is a tool rather than an outcome. A sensor network can produce large datasets while missing neighborhoods that lack infrastructure. Algorithms may reproduce biased inputs, and open data can expose private information if it contains detailed personal records. Governance and consent matter.

Define the decision before collecting data. If the purpose is to identify bus reliability, measure actual service intervals and passenger impact rather than only number of devices installed. Document coverage gaps and measurement errors. Maps should label source, coordinate system and dates. A predicted value must not be presented as a directly measured observation.

Maintain practical security and privacy controls for service and resident data. Sensitive infrastructure records require appropriate restrictions. Technology should remain usable by people without smartphones; nondigital access routes can be necessary for equitable public service.

**Fictional example:** A transit app shows on-time performance but excludes small buses not connected to its feed. The dashboard must identify its partial coverage.

**Practice:** Design one smart-city dashboard with three metrics and a disclosure of data gaps and privacy considerations.

## 10 — Urban Project Appraisal, Financing and Monitoring

Sustainable improvements require feasible delivery. A city may identify a need for sidewalks, shaded bus stops or drainage repairs, but actual budgets, maintenance, procurement and land permissions determine execution. Evaluate benefits, risks, costs, responsible agencies and how performance will be monitored. Choose interventions that improve well-defined needs rather than pursuing novelty alone.

A basic appraisal compares alternatives using transparent criteria. For transport, consider safety, access, travel time and operating cost. For green infrastructure, consider flood performance, maintenance and social use. Financial cost is not equivalent to economic or social value. Distributional effects matter: who receives the benefit and who may be adversely affected?

Set baselines and time periods before implementation. A post-project change may coincide with unrelated events, so avoid simple causal claims without a suitable evaluation design. Public reporting should be clear and allow correction of errors.

**Fictional example:** Three shaded bus stops cost $8,000 each, giving a nominal capital subtotal of $24,000 before land, design, installation contingencies or maintenance. The project cannot claim a complete budget from that one line.

**Practice:** Draft a six-item appraisal matrix with one uncertainty and one monitoring measure for each option.

## 11 — Worked Case: Improving a Fictional Neighborhood

**Entirely fictional teaching case.** A neighborhood has 1,000 households. Two hundred forty are beyond a specified transit-access threshold, 80% of waste is collected under the classroom measure, and a central street lacks continuous safe crossings. A small public square is poorly shaded and has limited accessibility. These are invented figures, not conditions in an identifiable real city.

Define goals around everyday access, service reliability and inclusive public space. Create a schematic map showing housing clusters, transit stops, walking routes, a service corridor and the square. Propose three actions: investigate safer crossings, improve route continuity, and study shade and accessibility upgrades for the square. Each action requires relevant professional verification before implementation.

**Calculations:** 240/1,000 = 24% of households lack convenient transit access under the invented rule. If 100 tonnes of waste are generated weekly and 80 tonnes collected, 20 tonnes are not in the controlled collection measure. Neither value proves the cause of a service gap or the effect of a proposed intervention.

Prepare a risk register and a transparent appraisal of who benefits. Include maintenance, accessibility, social cost, city agency responsibility, baseline and one-year monitoring. Do not call a shaded illustration a completed engineering design.

**Deliverables:** Brief, map, three project alternatives, evidence needs, indicators, cost assumptions, resident consultation plan and responsible monitoring proposal.

## 12 — Self-Test, Neighborhood Portfolio and References

**Self-test:** (1) What does SDG 11 aim to achieve? (2) Why does access require more than infrastructure count? (3) How do housing and transport planning interact? (4) Why is a sidewalk's connectivity important? (5) What is the difference between waste generation and controlled collection? (6) Why can a cooling intervention require local climate evidence? (7) What makes public space inclusive? (8) Why are digital city sensors not automatically representative? (9) Why must a project budget include maintenance? (10) What makes a monitoring indicator meaningful?

**Final project:** Develop an original improvement proposal for the fictional neighborhood. Provide a 250-word brief, diagram, three interventions with priorities, basic baseline indicators, stakeholder plan, six-line cost table, risk register, an accessibility review and a one-year monitoring plan. Mark every assumption and distinguish measured from simulated evidence.

**Practice rubric:** Urban-system understanding 20%; needs and equity 20%; mobility and services 20%; environment and resilience 15%; project feasibility 15%; transparent data and participation 10%. This is an educational exercise and does not certify an urban plan or a MAIC examination result.
