## 02 — Landscape Design: Site, Ecology and Experience

Landscape gardens combine planting, outdoor movement, terrain, water, materials and places to rest or gather. Digital visualization helps people compare concepts, but a beautiful rendered garden can hide drainage, accessibility or maintenance problems. Landscape design begins with the site and its users rather than selecting ornamental objects from a component library.

Learners will gather a site brief, document terrain and context, create simple landform geometry, use SketchUp's terrain approaches where available, plan paths and garden zones, model planting volumes and outdoor furnishings, test shade and views, compare material and water needs, prepare scenes and explain technical limitations. The result is a responsible schematic design, not a civil-engineering or landscape-architecture permit package.

**Eight-hour study allocation:** Orientation 20 minutes; eight topics on pages 3–10 at 45 minutes each (360 minutes); applied garden on page 11 for 60 minutes; final portfolio review on page 12 for 40 minutes. Total 480 minutes. A landscape sketch and simplified 3D model suffice for learning, regardless of whether professional survey data are available.

**Starter:** Draw a small garden that needs a shaded sitting area, a walking path and plant beds. Label sun, water and access questions before drawing decorative features.

## 03 — Site Survey, Climate and Design Brief

Collect evidence about site boundaries, elevation, slope, soil, sunlight, wind, utilities, existing trees, local water constraints and intended users. Aerial photographs or online terrain are useful starting references but can be inaccurate or outdated. Legal boundaries and infrastructure need reliable surveys before construction. Record which measurements are known and which are conceptual.

A brief should describe activities, budget, maintenance capacity, accessibility needs, climate and aesthetic direction. A family garden may prioritize safe play and low-maintenance planting, while a school garden may need education areas and clear paths. Do not treat a “green” appearance as proof that the proposal conserves water or supports local biodiversity.

Map the site's opportunities and risks. Low areas may collect water; steep slopes may complicate access; existing mature trees may provide valuable shade but need root-zone care. Choose appropriate specialist input for drainage, retaining structures and protected vegetation. Climate suitability of plant species should be verified locally, not inferred from a decorative SketchUp model.

**Fictional example:** A garden receives strong afternoon sun and has a low northeast corner. Before placing benches there, the team examines summer heat and possible ponding.

**Practice:** Write ten site survey questions, an activities list and a map of known and unknown conditions.

## 04 — Terrain, Contours and SketchUp Sandbox Concepts

Terrain is represented through elevations and surfaces. Contour lines connect points at equal height, while a triangulated irregular network (TIN) approximates a continuous surface using triangular faces. SketchUp documents Sandbox tools including From Contours, From Scratch and tools for shaping terrain. Availability and menu placement depend on the installed version or extension. These modeling tools do not replace survey precision.

Start with a flat base or an appropriately documented contour set. Create a simple surface and check that it remains usable for editing. Autodesk-style architectural solids and SketchUp terrain meshes serve different purposes; a terrain TIN is not a certified geotechnical model. Avoid unrealistic overhangs or folded terrain geometry. When moving vertices, record elevation changes and ensure the proposed grades can be assessed by qualified engineers.

Model gentle slopes to communicate circulation and planting ideas. A visually smooth ramp may still be too steep under the applicable accessibility requirements; do not declare it compliant based on a screenshot. Keep original terrain and proposed terrain as distinct model groups or versions.

**Fictional example:** A site has two contour lines showing a one-meter rise over an illustrative horizontal span. The preliminary model communicates grade only; actual excavation and drainage quantities need verified survey data.

**Practice:** Build a small fictional terrain mesh, document the assumed elevations and identify two potential modeling artifacts.

## 05 — Zones, Paths, Access and Outdoor Furniture

A landscape concept organizes outdoor zones: entry, circulation, planting, sitting, play or service. Paths connect the activities and should avoid unsafe conflicts with steps, irrigation and maintenance access. Consider wheelchair or stroller users, surface stability, transitions and lighting, while acknowledging that exact accessible-route rules depend on location and project use.

Model paths using meaningful widths, gradients and surfaces supported by site analysis. Decorative stepping stones may be attractive but not suitable as the only accessible route. Seats and tables require enough surrounding space to be approached and used. A pergola or retaining wall may need structural engineering rather than a simple visual component.

Use SketchUp groups or components to keep hardscape and furniture separate from raw terrain geometry. Compare two circulation schemes with the same site boundary. Review sightlines, shade and practical maintenance access, not only symmetry.

**Fictional example:** A central path crosses a planting bed and forces maintenance crews to enter through a seated group. A revised path gives separate access and reduces conflict.

**Practice:** Draw a zoning map and two path layouts. Identify where accessibility and safety checks remain unresolved.

## 06 — Plants, Soil, Water and Seasonal Design

Plant selection depends on climate, soil, water availability, mature size, root behavior, maintenance and ecological context. A rendered tree model can look appealing at planting time but may not represent future canopy spread or root interaction with structures. Use plant labels and realistic mature-space allowances. Avoid recommending invasive species without local review.

Water planning considers irrigation demand, rainfall, soil infiltration and drought periods. Native or climate-adapted planting may help reduce inputs in some projects, but actual performance depends on species and site conditions. Some lawns and ornamental water features need significant maintenance. Preserve existing vegetation when feasible and supported by arboricultural assessment.

A garden changes over seasons. A proposal should communicate spring, summer and winter conditions where relevant, and how deciduous or evergreen planting affects shade. Model a simplified plant palette with separate groups for trees, shrubs and ground cover. In a real project, document botanical names, sourcing, irrigation and establishment care.

**Fictional example:** A tree is placed beside a patio for shade. The drawing notes that its mature canopy and root-zone clearance are to be verified by a locally qualified planting specialist.

**Practice:** Prepare a five-plant schedule with hypothetical requirements and mark all details needing local confirmation.

## 07 — Materials, Drainage and Sustainable Choices

Hardscape choices affect durability, surface temperature, slip risk, water behavior and maintenance. A permeable-looking pattern is not proof of an engineered permeable pavement system. Actual permeability depends on the material structure, bedding, soil and maintenance. Avoid claiming rainwater retention from a model unless the design includes tested drainage calculations.

Think about runoff. Soil compaction, grading and surface coverage may change where rainwater flows. A water feature can create safety, energy and hygiene considerations. Retaining walls and major grade changes should be designed with qualified geotechnical or structural input. SketchUp can help visualize them, but cannot determine earth pressure or drainage capacity from appearance.

Use materials that suit the user and climate. High-gloss paving may be slippery when wet; dark surfaces may absorb more heat. Consider reuse of suitable materials while verifying contaminants and structural suitability. Compare total upkeep, not just initial visual appeal.

**Fictional example:** A concept swaps an impermeable patio surface for a permeable system. The proposal records an engineering investigation of local soil, subbase and drainage conditions before claiming benefits.

**Practice:** Compare two paving alternatives across safety, water, lifespan, maintenance and cost.

## 08 — Shadows, Seasonal Views and Landscape Visualization

Visualization helps clients understand spatial experience. SketchUp's views and shadow settings may support approximate lighting studies when location, time and orientation are set, but a rendering alone does not establish measured heat, microclimate or daylight performance. Model important plant masses at plausible scale rather than choosing oversized decorative assets that conceal paths.

Prepare views for different decisions. A plan view explains zoning and circulation; an eye-level view shows seating experience; an isometric view reveals terrain. A series of scenes can compare design alternatives, but should not hide drainage risks or access conflicts by selectively turning off objects. Keep camera positions consistent where possible.

Show changes over time carefully. Plants grow, and their mature form may affect views and structures. If the scene is illustrative, say so. A garden rendered with lush mature planting immediately after construction may create unrealistic expectations. Include a planting schedule and establishment note.

**Fictional example:** A proposed pergola creates an appealing shadow in a summer scene. Its structure, attachment and actual seasonal shading remain subject to design verification.

**Practice:** Produce three annotated scenes for plan, user-eye and terrain views, each identifying an unresolved site issue.

## 09 — Model Organization, Alternatives and Collaboration

Use named groups for terrain, existing conditions, paths, planting and furniture. Components are useful for repeated benches or lights. Tags and scenes can control review visibility, but do not remove objects from the actual design. Keep raw geometry organized to avoid attaching a path to a terrain face accidentally.

Preserve model versions before major changes such as altered grades. A design team may compare a flat plaza with a terraced scheme, but both should reference the same surveyed boundary. Document changes and their reasons. Avoid circulating conflicting renders and models without revision labels.

Collaborate with specialists when the model touches drainage, irrigation, civil works, utility clearance or safety. A model viewer may help them inspect the intention, yet they may require actual survey files, drawings and calculations. Create an issue log with responsible party, evidence needed and resolution status.

**Fictional example:** One alternative moves a path near a tree trunk. The model flags possible root-zone impact and requests arboricultural review rather than treating the new location as final.

**Practice:** Organize ten model objects, create two alternative scenes and record one interdisciplinary question.

## 10 — Deliverables, Estimate and Quality Review

A schematic landscape package should explain the brief, existing conditions, site plan, zoning, circulation, planting approach, materials, views, budget assumptions and unresolved technical checks. Keep source geometry editable and prepare appropriate annotated exports. The actual software edition may limit export or layout options, so verify compatibility with reviewers.

Cost estimates should include plants, hardscape, soil preparation, irrigation, labor, delivery, maintenance and contingency. A cost comparison based only on purchase prices omits installation and ongoing obligations. Use explicit units and quantities. A design promising “low maintenance” needs a defined plan and evidence, not merely a sparse-looking planting bed.

Before release, check dimensions, labels, model layers, hidden objects, plant scale, shadows, views and rights to visual assets. Confirm that the proposed paths connect logically and that unresolved drainage or structural work is clearly identified. A professionally drawn concept can still contain incorrect source dimensions.

**Fictional example:** Twenty shrubs at a hypothetical $15 each cost $300 before installation. If ten benches are proposed, their actual footprint and procurement cost should also be checked rather than inferred from the model.

**Practice:** Prepare a cost table, handoff checklist and one correction note for a discovered layout issue.

## 11 — Worked Case: A Small Community Garden

**Entirely fictional project.** A rectangular community garden site is assumed to measure 12 m × 8 m, giving a nominal area of 96 m². It needs a shaded sitting zone, a main path, planting beds and maintenance access. The site has an illustrative low corner, but no verified soil, drainage, utilities or survey. The proposal cannot be presented as a construction plan.

Create an existing-condition sketch and simple SketchUp site surface. Use a flat base first, then a modest hypothetical terrain variation. Model a path, group the sitting area and create planting zones as separate objects. Compare a direct central path with a perimeter path, noting the amount of space left for planting and the user movement pattern.

**Illustrative arithmetic:** If a nominal 20 m² is dedicated to sitting, a 16 m² path and 8 m² service area, 96 - 20 - 16 - 8 = 52 m² remains for other functions before accounting for exact shape and construction needs. This is not a real planting coverage or stormwater calculation.

Prepare a planting schedule with fictional labels, one neutral plan view and two visual scenes. List three local plant suitability questions, one accessibility concern and one drainage issue. Present a cost summary and a responsible recommendation that names required surveys and professional reviews.

**Deliverables:** Brief, site constraints, organized SketchUp model, terrain note, two path concepts, planting and material schedules, scenes, calculations and unresolved issue list.

## 12 — Review Questions, Landscape Portfolio and References

**Self-test:** (1) Why survey the site before designing? (2) What is a contour line? (3) What is a TIN? (4) Why should terrain not fold over itself? (5) How do paths support outdoor zoning? (6) Why is a rendered tree not a real mature-size specification? (7) What makes a paving system truly permeable? (8) Why can a shadow preview be misleading? (9) How should landscape model layers and versions be managed? (10) What technical reviews remain before construction?

**Portfolio task:** Prepare an original concept for the fictional community garden or another clearly identified imaginary site. Submit a brief and constraint map, simple terrain, two circulation alternatives, three planting zones, plant/material schedules, three annotated scenes, a cost assumption sheet and an issue register. State all verified facts separately from assumptions.

**Practice rubric:** Site understanding 20%; terrain and model quality 20%; zoning and circulation 20%; planting and environmental reasoning 15%; visualization 10%; cost, accessibility and review discipline 15%. This is a learning activity, not official landscape-architect licensure or construction approval.
