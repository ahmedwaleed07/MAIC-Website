## 02 — Orientation: What Journalists Do

Journalism answers questions that matter to a public. It gathers information, tests the reliability of that information, places findings in a meaningful context, and communicates them without distorting the evidence. Journalists do not simply repeat statements. They distinguish what a person said from what documents establish, what a reporter directly observed, and what remains unknown. The public benefit of a report depends on the quality of its verification and the relevance of its subject.

By the end of this introductory course, learners should be able to recognize a newsworthy question, distinguish facts from allegations and opinions, make a basic reporting plan, conduct neutral interviews, use accurate attribution, prepare a short news story and check it for fairness and avoidable harm. The emphasis is on decisions a beginner can practice rather than memorizing professional vocabulary.

**Suggested six-hour plan:** Orientation 20 minutes; pages 3–4, 60 minutes; pages 5–6, 60 minutes; pages 7–8, 60 minutes; pages 9–10, 60 minutes; reporting case page 11, 70 minutes; and final assignment page 12, 90 minutes. Total: 360 minutes. Reading, note-taking, verification simulations and revision all count as study time.

**Activity:** Choose one daily-life issue, such as a changed public transport timetable. Write one neutral question whose answer could be checked from records or firsthand observations. Explain why the answer could help people.

## 03 — Recognizing a Story and Its Audience

A useful news idea is more than an attention-grabbing event. Consider the size and nature of the public affected, the practical consequences, the timing, evidence of change and whether the story informs an important choice. A routine service notice may be valuable to residents even if it is not dramatic. A sensational claim may be unsuitable for publication if it cannot be supported with reliable evidence.

Start with a factual reporting question. Instead of "Why is the service terrible?" ask "What service was scheduled, what actually occurred, who was affected, and what explanation has been documented?" List what a reader needs to know first. Identify the difference between confirmed facts, promising leads, and guesses. Avoid selecting a headline before you know which statements can be verified.

News values such as impact, relevance, timeliness, proximity, conflict and novelty are tools for deciding what to investigate. None replaces factual accuracy or ethical judgment. A good reporting proposal identifies the public-interest purpose and a realistic way to test the claim. It also notes likely limits, such as documents that are unavailable or sources who have not replied.

**Fictional example:** A library announces extended weekend opening. Residents may need the exact dates, hours, services available and whether the change is permanent. The public information is more useful than an unverified claim that the entire library system has been transformed.

**Exercise:** Write two proposed headlines for the fictional announcement: one that can be supported immediately and one that would require additional evidence. Explain why the second is not yet publishable.

## 04 — Facts, Assertions, Opinions and Uncertainty

A fact is a claim that can be checked against appropriate evidence. An assertion is something someone states; it does not become verified merely because it is expressed forcefully. An opinion evaluates or interprets information. A prediction describes a possible future outcome rather than a completed event. These categories can appear together in a story, so the journalist needs language that preserves the distinctions.

Compare "The council approved the plan" with "The council is considering the plan." The first requires evidence of an adopted decision; the second may be supported by an agenda or formal proposal. A business promise of 100 future positions is not evidence that 100 employees have already been hired. An individual witness might accurately describe one experience without establishing how frequently an incident happens in the wider population.

Use careful terms when facts are incomplete. "According to the dated announcement" attributes the document; "residents said" reports a claim; "records reviewed by the newsroom show" describes examined evidence, if those records actually exist and have been inspected. Uncertainty is not a weakness to hide. It tells readers what they may rely on and what could change with new information.

**Fictional example:** A school plans to purchase 30 computers. Classify the following separately: the proposal, the vote approving funds, the purchase order and the delivered equipment.

**Exercise:** Write a fact, allegation, opinion and prediction about a fictional new bus route. List what evidence each would require before publication.

## 05 — Finding and Checking Sources

Sources include people, documents, original recordings, datasets, observable events and relevant technical expertise. Ask what each source can actually establish. A witness may describe an event they saw but cannot necessarily confirm its cause. A public agency may document its own decision but not every outcome of that decision. A specialist can explain a technical issue but may not know a specific disputed event firsthand.

Whenever feasible, corroborate significant facts independently. Two web pages that copied the same original post are not independent sources. Look for primary records: dated official notices, meeting decisions, audited statistics, original photographs, and direct responses. Check timestamps and versions. A screenshot can omit context, and an authentic photograph can be described with an incorrect date or location.

Create a simple source table with four fields: claim, source, verification status and remaining question. Distinguish "confirmed by original record" from "stated by one participant" and "not yet checked." Mark a claim as disputed where sources directly conflict and explain the nature of the conflict. Do not present a source as independent when it is financially or organizationally connected to the subject.

**Fictional example:** A club announces 500 attendees; a security estimate lists 350 and a ticketing report lists 420 entries. Before writing an attendance figure, clarify whether the records count admissions, tickets issued or people present.

**Exercise:** Make a source table for three claims arising from a fictional festival. Identify which source has direct knowledge of each specific claim.

## 06 — Notes, Interviews and Direct Observation

A reporter's notes should establish when and where information was obtained, by whom, under what conditions and with which remaining doubts. Record direct observations separately from explanations provided by sources. If you saw a street barrier, you can describe the barrier, but not assume the official reason for its installation. Verify spelling of names, positions, dates and figures before quoting them.

Prepare interview questions that seek information rather than agreement. Start with "What happened?" and "How do you know?" Then ask for precise times, relevant documents, alternative explanations and people who might confirm the account. Avoid questions that embed an accusation as a proven premise. Let the source answer, listen carefully, and check whether you understood the response. An interviewee's refusal to comment should be recorded accurately, not embellished.

Respect safety, privacy and applicable recording and consent obligations. Introduce yourself and the purpose of the interview. Give especially careful consideration to minors, vulnerable people and those discussing distressing experiences. If permission to quote or record is uncertain, clarify it before publication. Retain original notes and recordings according to suitable newsroom procedures.

**Fictional example:** Rather than asking "Why did the council neglect local residents?", ask "What requests were received, and how did the council respond? Which records show the dates?"

**Exercise:** Prepare six neutral interview questions for a hypothetical temporary power interruption. After each, name the type of evidence a good answer could provide.

## 07 — Understanding News Structure and the Lead

A news story should allow readers to understand the essential development quickly. A common structure places the most important verified information in the first paragraph, followed by relevant details, context, affected people and the responsible parties' responses. This is often called the inverted pyramid. It is not the only legitimate form, but it helps beginners avoid burying the central finding beneath generic background.

The "five Ws and one H" are who, what, when, where, why and how. Not every answer is known at publication time, and a lead should not invent one to appear complete. Start with the most useful confirmed elements; explain uncertainty honestly. Use simple, specific verbs. Keep the chronology consistent and separate a planned event from one already completed. Do not treat a source's interpretation as a confirmed causal explanation.

Each paragraph should help the reader answer a question. Follow up the lead with the scale of the event, evidence for important numbers, practical instructions and any meaningful limitations. A quote should add direct evidence or insight, not replace verification. Provide enough attribution that the reader understands who claims what.

**Fictional example:** "The town's main library will remain open until 7 p.m. on Saturdays beginning next month, according to a dated announcement issued on Monday." This states a plan, not an accomplishment.

**Exercise:** Write a 30-word lead from a fictional service notice. Underline each claim and name the evidence supporting it.

## 08 — Accurate Headlines, Quotes and Captions

A headline summarizes what the story actually supports. It must not imply a conclusion stronger than the body. Avoid emotionally loaded adjectives, invented causal claims and categorical descriptions of allegations. If officials propose a change, do not headline it as a completed change. If an arrest occurred, do not turn it into a conviction. Labels can affect reputations and readers' understanding, so precision matters.

Quotation marks indicate actual words from a source or documented material. Never place paraphrase inside quotation marks or invent a quotation that would sound believable. Confirm important quotes against notes or recordings. A fair paraphrase preserves the source's intended meaning and relevant qualifications. Direct speech may be shortened only without changing substance or misleading context, and according to applicable editorial practice.

Captions also make factual claims. An image should be labeled with accurate location, date and context when those details matter. Reusing an old photograph without clear labeling can make readers believe it depicts a current event. Crops and edited audio can remove essential context. Verify the original where possible and disclose material alterations appropriately.

**Fictional example:** A meeting agenda names a proposal for new cycle lanes. "Council considers cycle-lane proposal" is supportable; "Council has built new cycle lanes" is not.

**Exercise:** Correct three overstated headlines about a proposal, an unverified allegation and an early forecast. Write a caption that explicitly states the image is an illustrative file photograph.

## 09 — Fairness, Independence, Privacy and Corrections

Accuracy involves both the statements and how the full report presents them. Leaving out a key qualification can mislead even if a sentence is literally true. Include material context, seek relevant responses to serious allegations and avoid hiding evidence merely because it challenges the expected angle. Fairness does not mean treating all claims as equally supported: stronger evidence should be described as stronger evidence.

Journalists should avoid conflicts that could distort a story. Financial relationships, gifts or sponsored arrangements may compromise independence or create a reasonable appearance of bias. Advertising and promotion must be clearly separated from editorial reporting. Sensitive personal information should not be published automatically just because it is accessible. Balance public interest against foreseeable privacy and safety risks; legal rules may differ by jurisdiction.

Mistakes require transparent corrections. When a published figure, name or location is materially wrong, correct it promptly, state what changed and preserve a reasonable record of the update. Do not silently replace a significant error and pretend it was never present. Correcting new evidence does not imply that all previous reporting was dishonest; it is a visible part of accountability.

**Fictional example:** A notice states 14 approved street stalls, but an article reports 41. The correction should give the verified number and identify the earlier mistake.

**Exercise:** Write a two-sentence correction. Then identify three personal details that could be unnecessary in a public-service story.

## 10 — Digital Publication and Basic Verification

A public-service article may be distributed as website text, a short social post, an audio update or a captioned video. Short formats do not reduce the obligation to be accurate. A summary must preserve any important "planned," "alleged," "estimated," or "not yet confirmed" wording. Distinguish what a video shows from what an uploader claims happened. Check the original upload, date, location and whether the clip has been edited or relabeled.

Before reporting a viral claim, ask who first published it and what independent evidence exists. Do not infer authenticity solely from large numbers of views. Reverse-image research and source tracing can provide leads, but a result still needs interpretation and confirmation. An original image can be genuine yet unrelated to the claimed place or time. Artificially generated media or manipulated recordings must not be represented as actual documentary evidence.

AI tools can help organize notes and transcribe audio but may mishear or invent text. A journalist must check the tool's output against the original record, especially names, numbers and quotations. Clear corrections, source references and accessible captions help an audience distinguish reliable updates from speculation. Popularity is not a substitute for public value.

**Fictional example:** A viral picture appears to show a new stadium fire. The photo is from an older, unrelated event. A responsible report would not publish the new-event claim without verified context.

**Exercise:** Write a five-step verification checklist for a social-media photograph, then adapt it for a 15-second video.

## 11 — Worked Beginner Reporting Case

**Entirely fictional classroom simulation.** A public library publishes a notice that it will open on Sunday for an experimental six-week period. The notice states that ordinary borrowing services will be available, but says nothing about computers, study-room bookings or whether the trial will continue afterward. A local social post announces that the library has "permanently switched to seven-day operation." Your assignment is to draft a short useful report.

First, read the original notice and record the issuing organization, publication date, first Sunday covered, hours and stated duration. Then identify what is not confirmed: permanent opening, special facilities, future budget and staffing. You may quote the social post as a claim if it serves a specific public-interest purpose, but you must not repeat its unsupported "permanent" assertion as fact. Ask the library for clarification; do not invent an answer if it has not responded.

**Model lead:** "The town library will test Sunday opening for six weeks beginning next month, according to a dated notice published by the library." This wording is justified only by the fictional facts supplied in the exercise. A second paragraph could explain the announced hours and borrowing services, then note the unanswered question about other facilities. A useful publication should tell residents where to find the latest official schedule without inventing a permanent change.

**Application:** Build a claim-source table; write a 90-word news update; draft two neutral questions for the library; and identify the mistake that would require an immediate correction if the library gave a different start date.

## 12 — Review Questions, Applied Task and Rubric

Answer the following questions without consulting the text, then use the relevant section to check your reasoning. (1) What makes a question newsworthy? (2) What is the difference between a fact and an allegation? (3) Why does a screenshot not always prove an event? (4) What information belongs in a source log? (5) How do you change a leading interview question into a neutral one? (6) What does a news lead usually provide? (7) Why should headlines not overstate evidence? (8) What should accompany a reused file photograph? (9) How should a material factual error be corrected? (10) Why are social-media engagement counts not proof of accuracy?

**Practical task:** Prepare a 300- to 400-word fictional local news report using the library-opening case or another clearly identified hypothetical service change. Submit a short story proposal, claim-and-source table, six interview questions, a factual headline, a lead and two paragraphs of verified details. Add a 100-word editorial note identifying uncertainties, privacy considerations and a method for publishing corrections. If using real public records for learning, identify the original sources accurately rather than inventing confirmations.

**Self-assessment rubric:** Evidence and verification 30%; clear reporting question 15%; accurate lead and structure 20%; attribution and fairness 15%; ethics and corrections 10%; usefulness and presentation 10%. A score is a practice aid, not an official assessment outcome or proof of academy certification. Revise weak evidence before moving on.
