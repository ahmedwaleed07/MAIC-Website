## 02 — Infographics as Evidence-Based Visual Stories

An infographic combines information, numbers, labels and graphics to help an audience understand a subject. It is not a poster filled with icons, nor does it become accurate simply because the design looks polished. An effective infographic answers a useful question, identifies its sources, chooses an appropriate representation and provides enough context that readers are not misled. Graphic choices influence how large, urgent or certain a claim appears.

Learners should be able to define an audience and question, collect and inspect a small dataset, distinguish counts from percentages, choose chart types, establish a clear visual hierarchy, write accurate labels, develop an original visual style, provide accessible text equivalents, and verify an output before publishing. The approach is project-centered: each section produces a component of a final evidence-based graphic.

**Guided six-hour route:** Orientation 20 minutes; eight technique sections on pages 3–10 at 35 minutes each (280 minutes); applied infographic build on page 11 for 45 minutes; and final review on page 12 for 15 minutes. Total 360 minutes, including source checks and exercise decisions. Projects with more data or multilingual needs may take longer.

**Starter exercise:** Choose one question about a fictional community program: “How many sessions were held each month?” Write the audience, units, time period and evidence source you would need. Do not create a chart before defining those items.

## 03 — The Brief, Audience and Editorial Question

A visual project begins with a brief identifying the public or organizational decision it supports. Ask what the reader should know or do after seeing the graphic. A transport-service update may require a route and schedule; a budget explanation may require figures and category definitions; a training report may need results over several time periods. The title should express the established subject, not promise a dramatic finding that the data cannot support.

A useful infographic brief records the audience, topic, central question, source documents, publication channel, size, language and deadline. It also lists risks: misleading comparisons, missing values, confidential information and production limitations. Identify whether the graphic is explanatory, comparative, procedural, geographical or chronological. A chart cannot answer an unclear question, no matter how elaborate its colors.

Establish an evidence threshold before publishing. An observed count from an actual register differs from an estimate, forecast or claim made by an individual. Label hypothetical datasets explicitly during training. Choose the smallest set of figures that answers the question honestly; avoid adding unrelated statistics only to occupy space.

**Fictional example:** A library wants to show monthly workshop attendance. The brief specifies participants counted at entry, January through March, three source logs and an accessible social graphic. It does not claim that attendance proves learning outcomes.

**Practice:** Draft a 100-word brief and write one editorial headline that states only what the source can establish.

## 04 — Data Sources, Cleaning and Verification

An infographic is as trustworthy as its data path. Preserve the original record, note its issuer, collection period, units and definitions, and create a working copy for analysis. A spreadsheet row might represent a participant, an event or a payment; confusing these units can multiply counts incorrectly. Check duplicates, missing observations, inconsistent dates and values recorded under different categories. Keep a short change log for any corrections or exclusions.

Do not silently fill missing values with zeros. Zero can mean an actual observation, while a blank may mean data was never collected. Different denominators can make two percentages incomparable. Before showing a trend, verify whether the collection method remained consistent. If one month's attendance record excludes online sessions and another includes them, a line chart may describe changes in counting rather than real participation.

Simple arithmetic should be reproducible. For a rate, state both numerator and denominator. For a comparison, use the same unit and time basis. For a forecast, distinguish expected values from measured outcomes. A credible infographic can include a note describing data limitations instead of hiding them.

**Fictional example:** A training center has 40 registered participants but only 32 verified attendees. A chart about attendance must count 32, not 40, unless it is explicitly labeled registration.

**Practice:** Inspect a hypothetical table with a duplicate record, an unknown attendance value and a different date format. Record how each issue should be handled.

## 05 — Choosing the Right Chart and Representation

A chart type should match the comparison. A bar chart is often effective for comparing distinct categories; a line chart commonly helps show a measure across ordered time periods; a scatter plot can illustrate a relationship between two numeric variables. A table may be preferable when readers need exact values rather than a general pattern. Maps require credible geographic data and should not imply precision beyond the available location records.

Avoid chart types simply because they look sophisticated. Three-dimensional bars can distort perceived height and make comparison harder. Pie charts may be useful for a few clearly defined parts of a whole but become difficult when there are many categories or nearly equal slices. If categories overlap, a part-of-whole graphic may be conceptually invalid. Every chart needs a title, labeled units, sensible scale and a clear description of what is included.

Match the chart to the claim. If the question is which course had the most attendees, bars with common zero baseline help readers compare counts. If the question concerns progression from January to June, a line can show ordered changes. A correlation between advertising and enrollment should not be labeled as proof that advertising caused every registration.

**Fictional example:** Workshop attendance for three months is 20, 30 and 25. A simple bar or line presentation can show the values, but the note must define whether they count people, visits or registrations.

**Practice:** Choose chart forms for categorical totals, an ordered time trend, a process sequence and an exact price table. Justify each choice.

## 06 — Scales, Axes, Percentages and Misleading Design

Visual scale determines how large a difference appears. When a bar chart compares magnitudes, a zero baseline is generally important because readers interpret bar lengths directly. Cutting the axis near the values can exaggerate a modest difference. A line chart may use a restricted range in appropriate contexts, but the scale and reason should be unmistakable. Distinguish absolute change from percentage change, and show units.

Calculate comparisons carefully. If attendance grows from 20 to 30, the absolute increase is 10 people and the relative increase is 10 divided by 20, or 50 percent. It would be incorrect to call that a 10 percent increase. Likewise, 30 percent of a sample is not automatically 30 percent of a larger population. Clearly identify the denominator, relevant period and data quality limitations.

Color, area and pictograms can also mislead. Enlarging both the height and width of an icon changes its area faster than its height, making values appear disproportionately different. Avoid “people” symbols that accidentally imply fractional individuals without explanation. A visually striking headline should not replace the measured conclusion.

**Fictional example:** Two programs have 100 and 110 attendances. Starting the bar axis at 95 could make the second appear several times greater, even though the numeric difference is ten.

**Practice:** Sketch a misleading comparison, then correct its baseline, units and explanatory caption. Calculate one absolute and relative change.

## 07 — Visual Hierarchy, Annotation and Narrative Flow

An infographic needs a reading route. Use a clear headline, short contextual introduction, primary visual finding, supporting comparisons and a source or method note. The hierarchy may be vertical on a phone and more spacious on desktop, but it should not lose important qualifications. Size and contrast can signal importance, while consistent alignment and white space keep groups intelligible.

Annotations explain what is noteworthy without inventing certainty. A label saying “Attendance increased after a scheduling change” reports a chronology if the dates match. It does not prove the schedule caused the increase. Use language such as observed, estimated and reported when the evidence requires it. Distinguish key takeaways from contextual details, and avoid restating every number in oversized decorative callouts.

A narrative sequence can support complicated information. For a process infographic, show the stages in a direction the audience can follow, and label dependencies. For a chronology, dates should proceed in the correct order. Images and icons may reinforce recognition but should not carry the only source of important meaning.

**Fictional example:** A public information graphic asks “How to register for a workshop.” The main steps are choose a session, complete the form and receive confirmation. Each stage includes one action and no unsupported claims.

**Practice:** Create a thumbnail showing a headline, one chart, two annotations, a limitations note and a source line. Ask a reader what they notice first.

## 08 — Color, Typography, Symbols and Originality

Choose a constrained visual vocabulary that clarifies relationships. A chart may use one color for the main series and a contrasting accent for a comparison, but viewers should not be forced to distinguish important categories using color alone. Add labels, patterns or other redundant cues when appropriate. Keep numeric and axis labels legible at final screen or print size. Avoid pale low-contrast text placed over a detailed photograph.

Typography should support rather than compete with data. A short title may use a distinctive display typeface; values, captions and source notes benefit from clear readable fonts. Use consistent type scales, alignment and spacing. In multilingual versions, test translated strings, numerals and right-to-left text appropriately while preserving approved brand wordmarks. If a long label wraps, redesign the available space instead of shrinking every letter until unreadable.

Create original diagrams or use rights-cleared assets. A competitor's proprietary infographic is not a blank template simply because it appears publicly online. Reuse of another producer's icon set, chart or photograph may require permission. Generate symbols only when they represent the subject accurately and make no false documentary claims.

**Fictional example:** The same attendance values are represented with blue bars and direct text labels, so the chart remains interpretable even if printed in grayscale.

**Practice:** Produce a six-color or smaller palette, type hierarchy and symbol legend for an original infographic. Test a monochrome version.

## 09 — Accessibility and Text Equivalents

A visual chart may be inaccessible to someone who cannot see the image or who needs magnification. WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 1.1.1 addresses non-text content and meaningful text alternatives in its web scope. For complex graphs, a short accessible label may need to be accompanied by a longer description or an accessible data table that communicates the essential relationships and numbers. A chart title alone is not an adequate substitute for the meaning of a complex diagram.

Write descriptions that convey information rather than decoration. “Bar chart” identifies the form but not the result; “Workshop attendance rose from 20 in January to 30 in February, then fell to 25 in March” communicates the relevant trend. Include units and meaningful caveats. The appropriate alternative depends on whether the exact numbers are necessary for a task. Do not bury critical data only in a screenshot that cannot be enlarged or read by assistive technology.

Test contrast, reading order, zoom and legibility on narrow screens. A color-coded map should also provide location names or other accessible cues. Interactive infographics need usable controls and appropriate keyboard behavior if they are part of a website. An exported static file may need a separate accessible companion page.

**Fictional example:** A five-category chart has a short alt label plus a nearby HTML data table listing the exact category totals. The text alternative remains faithful to the visible values.

**Practice:** Write a 25-word short description and a 100-word extended explanation for a hypothetical chart, preserving all significant data.

## 10 — Production, Review and Multiformat Delivery

An infographic workflow moves from brief to source verification, visual sketch, draft, review, revised design and final export. Record who checked the data and which file was used. Do not allow color correction or template changes to alter actual labels. If a data source is updated, identify every graphic and caption that depends on it. A project is ready for review when its values can be traced back to the source.

Prepare the layout for actual destinations: an A4 printable explanation, a vertical phone graphic or a website element may require different arrangements. Resize or stack groups while keeping hierarchy and facts intact. Avoid cropping a source footnote when converting a desktop graphic into a mobile story. Export appropriate formats based on use and verify the actual output outside the design application.

Final checks should cover arithmetic, units, axis scales, category labels, legends, accessibility alternatives, rights and date of the data. Look for truncated text, mixed decimal conventions, misleading annotations and unreadable small captions. Retain an editable source and a dated change log. If a published value is materially wrong, issue a corrected version and an explanation rather than silently changing the source.

**Fictional example:** An A4 infographic appears correct, but its mobile crop hides the denominator note. The mobile version must be recomposed and checked before sharing.

**Practice:** Create a release checklist with separate content, accessibility and technical checks. Document one correction after an export test.

## 11 — Worked Case: A Community Workshop Attendance Graphic

**Entirely fictional dataset.** A community center holds monthly learning sessions and records verified attendances: January 20, February 30, March 25 and April 35. The units are attendances, not necessarily distinct people. The source is a fictional attendance ledger created for this exercise. No real institution, survey or performance result is implied.

Define the question “How did recorded workshop attendance change across four months?” Use a bar or line chart with a clearly labeled month axis, attendance values and a transparent numeric scale. The observed increase from January to April is 15 attendances, equivalent to 15/20 = 75 percent relative to January. The change from February to March is a decrease of 5, or 5/30 ≈ 16.7 percent. Neither trend proves that advertising or management decisions caused the changes.

Create a second mobile layout and make every value legible. Include a brief description of the source and method. A suitable text alternative could state: “Monthly attendances were 20, 30, 25 and 35 from January through April, with April the highest.” Consider whether viewers need the full values in an accessible data table. Do not place the source note outside a phone crop.

**Project outputs:** One-page brief, original data sheet, chart-selection rationale, two thumbnail concepts, full chart with labels, mobile adaptation, alt text, and a final QA report. Add a correction scenario in which March's count was entered as 52 rather than 25; identify every number, caption or conclusion affected.

**Self-check:** Explain both the absolute and percentage changes using the correct denominators. Identify one unsupported causal conclusion that the graphic must not make.

## 12 — Assessment, Portfolio and Reference Material

**Review questions:** (1) What distinguishes an infographic from decoration? (2) What belongs in a visual communication brief? (3) Why should source data be preserved? (4) How do counts differ from percentages? (5) When is a bar chart appropriate? (6) Why can a truncated bar axis mislead? (7) How does a visual hierarchy guide reading? (8) Why should important groups have labels beyond color? (9) What can a text alternative for a complex chart include? (10) What should be checked after exporting a graphic?

**Final portfolio task:** Create an original one-page infographic explaining the fictional four-month attendance ledger, plus a narrow-screen version. Include the title, source and period, data table, clearly labeled chart, two responsible annotations, a short/long accessible description and a correction log. Provide a short rationale for the chart type, zero baseline choice if using bars, type scale and accessibility decisions. Use only fictional or approved source data and appropriate rights-cleared assets.

**Practice rubric:** Question and audience fit 15%; data checks and arithmetic 25%; chart suitability and truthful scaling 20%; coherent hierarchy and originality 15%; accessible equivalents and readability 15%; export and documented corrections 10%. This is a learning rubric, not an official examination result, degree or guaranteed certification.
